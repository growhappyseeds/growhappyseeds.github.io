This guide is for the apartment with no balcony: a window or two, a radiator under one of them, and no outdoor space you are allowed to use. The constraint is light, because an indoor pot gets a fraction of what a garden bed gets. Kale handles that limit better than most vegetables.

Red Russian, the kale in the Happy Seeds kit, is the worked example below: a flat-leaved, purple-stemmed kale that tolerates cool rooms and repeated cutting. The plan is a deep pot, the brightest window you have, a cool spot away from the radiator, and scissors.

## Can you grow kale indoors from seed?

Yes, kale grows indoors from seed, and it is one of the easiest vegetables to grow in an apartment. Illinois Extension lists kale among the [leafy greens it recommends seeding into containers for winter](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2026-02-20-beat-winter-blues-growing-some-leafy-greens), and it needs no transplanting, no pollination and no trellis.

Expect a window plant, not a garden plant. Gardener's Path puts an outdoor Red Russian at up to 3 feet tall; an indoor one stays smaller and gives a steady supply of tender leaves rather than armfuls. The series post on [five vegetables for a windowsill](/blog/windowsill-vegetables-from-seed/) puts kale first for the same reason.

## How much light does indoor kale need?

Indoor kale needs a south or west window with direct sun for much of the day, or a lamp running 10 to 12 hours a day. Vermont Extension's guide to [growing greens in winter](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-greens-winter) gives that 10-to-12-hour figure and a spot right in front of a south or west window. It adds a lamp when the sun falls short.

In an east or north window, or anywhere from November to February, plan on the lamp. A Maryland extension expert answering a question about [kale seedlings stalling indoors](https://ask.extension.org/kb/faq.php?id=927038) advised keeping the LED light 4 to 6 inches above the seedlings. Raise it as the plants grow.

The series posts on [which window direction is best](/blog/best-window-direction-for-growing-vegetables/) and [grow lights for apartment seedlings](/blog/grow-lights-for-apartment-seedlings/) cover judging your exposure and a basic lamp setup.

## What pot and potting mix does kale need indoors?

Kale needs a pot about 10 inches deep with a drainage hole, filled with ordinary potting mix, not garden soil. Gardener's Path's guide to [Red Russian kale](https://gardenerspath.com/plants/vegetables/grow-red-russian-kale/) suggests a container at least a foot across and 10 inches deep for a full-size plant.

You can go shallower if you harvest young; the baby-leaf row below is usual container practice rather than a cited figure.

| Goal | Container | Depth | Plants per container | First cut |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baby leaves, cut and come again | Window box or wide tray | About 6 inches | One every few inches | About 25 days |
| Full-size leaves from one plant | Round pot, about 12 inches across | 10 inches | One | About 50 days |

Put a saucer under every pot, and a tray under the saucer if you rent. The series post on [where to start seeds in a small apartment](/blog/starting-seeds-in-a-small-apartment/) has the layout.

## How do you sow kale seeds indoors?

Sow kale seeds a quarter to a half inch deep in damp potting mix, two or three per spot. Keep the pot at 65 to 70 degrees F until they sprout. Iowa State University Extension's table of [germination requirements for vegetables](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/germination-requirements-annuals-and-vegetables) gives kale that temperature and 4 to 7 days to germinate. It lists kale as a seed that wants darkness, which the covering of mix provides.

Utah State University Extension's [kale guide](https://extension.usu.edu/yardandgarden/research/kale-in-the-garden.php) gives the same depth and says to thin seedlings at 3 to 4 true leaves. Snip the extras at soil level so the keeper's roots stay put.

1. Fill the pot to an inch below the rim with potting mix and water it until the mix is evenly damp.
2. Make holes a quarter to a half inch deep, a few inches apart for baby leaves or one in the center for a single plant.
3. Drop two or three seeds in each hole, cover, and press the surface gently.
4. Cover the pot loosely with a clear bag or lid until sprouts appear, then remove it and move the pot into full light the same day.
5. Thin to the strongest seedling per spot at 3 to 4 true leaves.

## What temperature does kale want indoors?

Kale wants a cool room, 60 to 75 degrees F, which most apartments supply without effort. The Maryland extension answer above names that range and calls temperature the limiting factor when seedlings stall. Utah State adds that growth is best when temperatures do not exceed 75 degrees F.

The danger spot is the sill directly above a steam radiator, where afternoon air can run far past that. Keep the pot a few feet from the heat or on a shelf to the side of the window. The series post on [radiators and indoor seedlings](/blog/radiator-heat-and-indoor-seedlings/) explains the two-stage plan: warmth to germinate, then cool bright air to grow.

## How do you water and feed indoor kale?

Water indoor kale when the top inch of mix is dry, enough that a little runs into the saucer, then empty the saucer. Utah State warns that swings between wet and dry make the leaves tough and the flavor off. A finger in the mix every other day beats a schedule.

Feeding is light. Most potting mixes carry enough fertilizer for the first month; after that, a half-strength liquid vegetable fertilizer every two to three weeks is plenty. More pushes soft growth that aphids love.

## When and how do you harvest indoor kale?

Harvest indoor kale by cutting the outer leaves with scissors and leaving the center to keep growing, from about four weeks after sowing. Red Russian is listed at 50 days to mature leaves by Utah State, and Gardener's Path puts baby greens at about 25 days.

Utah State's method is to strip the older leaves first so the young ones keep developing. Take no more than a third of the plant at one cut. When new leaves come in small and the stem gets woody, start a fresh pot.

Sow a new pot every four to six weeks and you will rarely be without kale. Utah State also notes that summer heat gives kale bitter or off flavors, one more reason to keep the pot cool.

## What goes wrong with kale indoors?

The common indoor kale problems are stretching from low light, aphids on soft new growth, and fungus gnats from mix kept too wet. Tall, pale, floppy seedlings mean the window is not enough; the fix is in the series post on [leggy seedlings](/blog/leggy-seedlings-indoors/). Aphids cluster under young leaves and wash off under the kitchen tap, repeated every few days.

Fungus gnats hover over mix that never dries; let the top inch dry between waterings and they fade out. Yellow lower leaves on a healthy plant are usually the oldest leaves finishing, so cut and eat them.

If you later get a balcony or a community plot, Red Russian goes outside too; on a balcony, check your [lease and building rules](/blog/balcony-garden-lease-and-building-rules/) and the weight limit first. Direct sow it 4 weeks before your last spring frost, or 8 to 10 weeks before the first fall frost, and confirm those dates with your county extension office. The [state planting calendars](/planting/) give the frost window to count from.
