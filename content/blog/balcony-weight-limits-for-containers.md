This guide is for the apartment gardener with a balcony, terrace or roof deck, where the limit is not light but weight. A balcony is a slab or a deck hanging off the side of a building, and a vegetable garden in big pots is heavy, wet and permanent.

Here is what a wet planter actually weighs, what the code assumes the balcony carries, where heavy pots should sit, and how to cut the weight. For sun hours, wind and pot sizes by crop, start with [the best vegetables to grow on a balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/).

## How much does a wet planter weigh?

A wet planter weighs several times what the empty pot and the bag of mix suggest, because water is the heavy part. The University of Maryland Extension's guide to [growing vegetables in containers](https://www.extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-vegetables-containers-and-salad-tables) says a 20-inch container filled with moist potting mix and plants can weigh 100 pounds. A gallon of water weighs about 8.3 pounds, and a thorough watering leaves a lot of it in the mix.

The table gives rough working figures for a peat-based mix watered until it drains, plus a plastic pot. They are estimates, not measurements of your pots, so weigh one container on a bathroom scale after watering.

| Container | Mix it holds | Rough weight, wet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Small pot, about 2 gallons | 0.3 cubic feet | 15 to 25 pounds |
| 5-gallon bucket or pot | 0.7 cubic feet | 30 to 45 pounds |
| 10-gallon tub | 1.3 cubic feet | 60 to 90 pounds |
| 20-inch pot, about 15 gallons | 2 cubic feet | about 100 pounds |
| 2 by 3 foot planter, 10 inches deep | 5 cubic feet | 250 to 400 pounds |

Terracotta, ceramic and concrete pots add their own weight on top, and a mix with garden soil in it weighs far more. Maryland Extension puts soil at about 75 pounds per cubic foot against about 10 for dry soilless media. Four warm-season vegetables in 5-gallon pots plus one melon tub weigh roughly as much as two adults, standing there all summer.

## How much weight can a balcony hold?

US building codes commonly design a residential balcony for 40 pounds per square foot, spread evenly over the whole surface. The current International Building Code table sets exterior balconies and decks at the same live load as the occupancy they serve. For private rooms in multifamily buildings that is 40 pounds per square foot, as in Massachusetts' [copy of Table 1607.1](https://regulations.justia.com/states/massachusetts/780-cmr/chapter-16/section-1607/subsection-1607-1).

The same table designs a roof used as a roof garden for 100 pounds per square foot, while an ordinary roof is not designed for people and pots. A 4 by 8 foot balcony at 40 pounds per square foot is designed for about 1,280 pounds spread across all 32 square feet. That total includes you, your guests and the furniture, not just the garden.

Treat the code figure as a design minimum, not as your balcony's real capacity. Older code editions asked more of balconies, and local amendments, the building's age, water damage and the way the balcony is supported all change the answer. The building's drawings say what was designed, and only a structural engineer can confirm what a particular balcony will carry today.

## Where should heavy pots sit on a balcony?

Heavy pots belong against the wall where the balcony meets the building, spread out rather than clustered. The Royal Horticultural Society's advice on [roof gardens and balconies](https://www.rhs.org.uk/advice/profile?PID=674) says heavy containers should be placed near load-bearing walls or over a load-bearing beam or joist. On a cantilevered balcony, the kind with no posts underneath, the building wall is the supported edge and the outer railing is the free end.

The horticulturist Stephen Albert makes the same point in his [balcony and rooftop garden basics](https://harvesttotable.com/balcony-and-rooftop-vegetable-garden-basics/): balconies are often suspended from the building and cannot bear heavy weight. On a balcony carried by posts, the corners beside the posts are the strong spots. The middle of the span and a cluster of big tubs at the outer edge are the places to avoid.

Keep pots off the railing unless the building allows it and the bracket is rated for a full, wet box, because a planter over a sidewalk is a falling hazard. Never put pots on a fire escape, because it is an emergency exit and blocking it breaks fire code in most cities.

## How do you lighten a balcony container garden?

Switch to a soilless mix, lighter pots and the smallest container each crop actually needs. The University of Wisconsin Extension's [container gardening guide](https://barron.extension.wisc.edu/files/2023/02/Container-Gardening-1.pdf) recommends soilless mixes where many containers sit on a balcony or similar structure and excess weight can be a problem. Peat or coir with perlite or vermiculite is light when dry and still lighter than soil when wet.

Fabric grow bags, plastic and resin pots weigh a fraction of terracotta or concrete, and Maryland Extension notes that fabric bags are lightweight and portable. Skip the layer of gravel in the bottom of the pot, because it adds weight without improving drainage. Empty saucers after watering so you are not storing gallons of water on the slab.

Then size each pot to the crop instead of buying the biggest tub on the shelf. Maryland Extension's minimum volumes run from half a gallon for beets to 5 gallons for standard tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and eggplant. Bigger pots hold more water and crop more reliably, but every gallon of mix above the minimum is weight the balcony carries all season.

| Crop in the Happy Seeds kit | Weight on the balcony | Where to put it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kale, spinach, turnip, beet | Light; several share one planter | Anywhere, or indoors on a sunny sill |
| Carrot | Medium; a 12-inch-deep pot holds more mix | Near the wall, or indoors |
| Pepper | Medium | Mid-balcony is fine |
| Tomato, eggplant, cucumber | Heavy once staked, trellised and watered | Against the building wall |
| Melon | Heaviest single pot, 10 gallons or more | At the wall, never at the outer edge |

## What do the lease and the building rules say about planters?

Read the lease, the house rules or the condo bylaws before buying a single pot, because many already limit what a balcony may hold. Common rules cover planters on or over the railing, drip onto the balcony below, and a weight limit that management will quote if you ask. A note to the super that you are adding large containers costs nothing and starts the conversation about the load limit.

Neighbors matter as much as rules. Water that runs out of a 10-gallon tub lands on the balcony below, so keep a tray under anything large. A trellis or cage adds little weight but catches wind, which [the balcony post](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/) covers.
