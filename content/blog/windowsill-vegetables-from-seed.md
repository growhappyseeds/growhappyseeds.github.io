This guide is for the apartment with a decent window and no balcony: no patio, no roof access, and a fire escape does not count. The constraint is light and root room, because a sill gets a few hours of direct sun and holds a pot under a foot deep. Five vegetables are happy inside those limits.

They are kale, spinach, beets grown for their greens, turnip greens and carrots in a deep pot. Each gets a section below with pot depth, sowing and harvest timing, and the last section explains why tomatoes are not on the list.

## What vegetables can you grow from seed on a windowsill?

The vegetables that grow from seed on a windowsill are leafy greens and small roots, because they tolerate modest light and fit in a shallow pot. Illinois Extension's list of [leafy greens for winter growing](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/good-growing/2026-02-20-beat-winter-blues-growing-some-leafy-greens) includes kale, spinach, turnip greens and beet greens, all seeded straight into containers of potting mix. Carrots join only when the pot is deep enough.

The Old Farmer's Almanac's guide to [vegetables you can grow indoors](https://www.almanac.com/vegetables-you-can-grow-indoors) draws the same line. Greens get by on four or more hours of sun a day; fruiting and root crops want six to eight and usually need a lamp. That difference is why the windowsill list is short.

## How much light does a windowsill need for vegetables?

A windowsill grows vegetables when it gets several hours of direct sun, and a south or west window is the one most likely to deliver it. Vermont Extension's guide to [growing greens in winter](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/growing-greens-winter) suggests 10 to 12 hours of light a day and a spot right in front of a south or west window. It adds a lamp when the sun falls short, and keeps pots away from the hot draft of a radiator.

If seedlings stretch toward the glass and turn pale, the window is not enough on its own. ## Kale: Red Russian on the sill

Kale is the most forgiving of the five, because it tolerates lower light, cooler rooms and repeated cutting. Red Russian has flat, purple-veined leaves that are tender when young. Sow seeds a quarter inch deep in a pot 6 to 8 inches deep, then thin to roughly one plant every 4 inches once the seedlings have true leaves.

Cut the lowest leaves first and leave the growing tip alone, the method Illinois Extension recommends for kale harvested repeatedly. When the stems get woody and the leaves small, sow a fresh pot.

## Spinach: Bloomsdale without the bolting

Spinach grows well in a window because it likes exactly what an apartment sill offers in the cool months: moderate light and a room that never gets hot. Illinois Extension notes that greens grow and taste better in cooler conditions and that spinach germinates best in cool soil. Keep the pot away from a radiator and it is far less likely to bolt than in a May garden, where heat and long days push it to seed.

Sow Bloomsdale a half inch deep, an inch apart, in a pot at least 6 inches deep, and thin to roughly 3 inches once the seedlings are up. Cut outer leaves as baby spinach from about three weeks on. A pot gives two or three cuttings, so sow another every three weeks.

## Beets: greens first, roots if the light allows

Beets earn a place on the sill for their leaves, which are ready weeks before any root forms and taste like a milder chard.

Maryland Extension's guide to [growing microgreens and baby greens indoors](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-microgreens-and-baby-greens-indoors) lists beets among its indoor greens. Its timeline puts baby leaves at 4 to 6 inches tall by day 21 to 40. A 6-inch pot is plenty for greens.

Every Chioggia "seed" is a cluster that sends up several seedlings, so thin to the strongest one roughly every 3 inches. If your window gives six or more hours of direct sun, leave a few plants in a 10-inch pot for small striped roots at 50 to 60 days. In a dimmer window, harvest the greens and be satisfied.

## Turnip greens: the fastest leaf on the list

Turnip greens are the quickest of the five to a usable harvest, with peppery leaves ready to cut about four to six weeks after sowing. Purple Top White Globe is grown for its tops as well as its roots, and the tops are the part a windowsill can deliver. Sow a quarter inch deep in a 6-inch pot and thin to roughly 2 or 3 inches apart.

Cut leaves when they are hand-sized and the plants keep producing for several weeks. Roots need a deeper pot, roughly 8 inches, and the same strong sun beets do, so treat them as a bonus.

## Carrots: the one that needs a deep pot and patience

Carrots grow on a windowsill only in a pot 12 inches deep, because Danvers 126 sends its root straight down and stops where the pot does.

Gardening Know How's guide to [growing carrots indoors](https://www.gardeningknowhow.com/edible/vegetables/carrot/indoor-carrot-growing.htm) puts standard-length carrots at 10 to 12 inches of soil in a sunny window. A tall, narrow pot on the floor under the sill works as well as one on it.

Carrots are the one root crop here and want the brightest spot you have, six or more hours of direct sun.

Scatter seed thinly on the surface, cover with a quarter inch of mix and keep it moist for the one to three weeks carrots take to sprout. Thin to about an inch apart, then to 2 inches once the tops are a few inches tall. Harvest from about 65 to 75 days by pulling one carrot straight up at a time.

## Why tomatoes are not a windowsill vegetable

A full-size tomato is not a windowsill crop because it needs direct sun for most of the day, a 5-gallon container and several feet of height. A sill supplies none of the three.

The Almanac's indoor guide rates even dwarf tomatoes as high-light plants in a large, deep pot. Floradade, the variety in the kit, is a full-size tomato that yields 75 to 80 days after transplant and wants real outdoor sun.

The same goes for the other warm-season packets: cucumber wants a 5-gallon pot and a trellis, pepper 3 to 5 gallons, eggplant 5 gallons and melon 10 gallons or more. The window is their nursery, not their home.

Sow tomato 6 to 8 weeks and pepper and eggplant 8 weeks before your last spring frost, and confirm that date with your county extension office.

After that the plant needs a balcony whose lease and building rules allow planters, a roof the building has approved in writing, or a community garden plot, never a fire escape.
