Picking the right crops matters more than any tool or fertilizer when you are growing vegetables from seed for the first time. Some vegetables sprout in three days and shrug off cold nights and missed waterings. Others need two months of babysitting indoors before they can even go outside.

The ranking below leans toward crops that forgive mistakes, because a first garden is as much about learning the rhythm of sowing and harvesting as it is about the harvest itself.

## The easiest vegetables to grow from seed, ranked

Radish, leaf lettuce, spinach, kale, beets and turnips are the easiest vegetables to grow from seed because they are sown directly in the soil, sprout in under two weeks, and produce food within one to two months. Warm-season fruiting crops are a step harder, mainly because of timing, not skill.

Carrots sit in the middle of the list not because they are fussy once growing, but because their seed is tiny and slow, often taking 14 to 21 days to appear. Beginners frequently give up on a carrot row or let it dry out in the second week. Keep the soil surface damp and they will come.

Melons are last because they need a long, hot season and a lot of room. In a short-summer state they are a stretch for a first garden; in Texas or inland California they are much easier. Check the window for your state on the [planting calendar pages](/planting/) before committing space to them.

## Why cool-season crops forgive mistakes

Cool-season crops forgive mistakes because they germinate in cool soil, tolerate light frost, and give you a harvest before summer heat and pests arrive. Spinach, kale, beets, turnips, radishes and lettuce can all go in the ground two to six weeks before your last spring frost.

That early window is a safety net. If a sowing fails, you have time to sow again, and in most of the country you get a second chance in late summer for a fall crop. Spinach will bolt (send up a flower stalk and turn bitter) once days get long and hot, so treat it as a spring and fall vegetable rather than a summer one.

Cool-season crops also tend to be eaten as leaves or roots, not fruit. You do not have to wait for flowers, pollination and ripening; you simply pick when the plant is big enough. That removes several of the steps where a warm-season crop can go wrong.

## What to start indoors (and what not to)

Start tomatoes, peppers and eggplant indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost, and direct sow nearly everything else. Those three crops need a long warm season that most of the US cannot supply if the seed goes straight in the ground in May.

Everything else on the beginner list prefers direct sowing. Root crops such as carrots, beets and turnips dislike being moved and fork or stall if their roots are disturbed. Beans, cucumbers, zucchini and melons grow so fast that an indoor head start saves little, and their seedlings resent transplanting.

If you do start seeds indoors, the keys are a bright light source a few inches above the seedlings, warmth for germination, and a week of hardening off outdoors before planting. The full method is in [How to Start Seeds Indoors](/blog/when-to-start-seeds-indoors/).

## The three mistakes beginners make

The three mistakes that end most first gardens are sowing seed too deep, overwatering, and planting warm-season crops before the soil has warmed. Each is easy to avoid once you know to look for it.

**Sowing too deep.** A seed should be covered by only 2 to 3 times its own width. Lettuce and carrot seed want barely a dusting of soil; bean and squash seed can go an inch down. Buried too deep, small seeds run out of energy before they reach the light, and the packet gets blamed for poor germination.

**Overwatering.** Seedlings need the soil evenly moist, not soggy. Constant wetness starves roots of oxygen and invites damping off, the fungal collapse of seedlings at the soil line. Water when the top half inch is dry, and water less often but more deeply as plants grow.

**Planting warm crops too early.** Tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and melons sulk or die in cold soil, even if the air feels warm. Wait until one to two weeks after your last frost date, when soil is reliably warm, and you will often catch up to or pass plants that went in a month earlier. ## A simple first-season plan

A good first garden has two or three quick greens, two or three root crops, and two or three warm-season fruiting crops, sown in that order across the spring. That spread teaches the full rhythm of a season while keeping the plant count manageable.
