This guide is for the apartment with a balcony, terrace or roof deck that faces south or west and bakes from noon until sunset. That exposure is the best light in the building, and it comes with a constraint: the air, the wall behind you and the pots themselves run hotter than the forecast. The workable answer is to keep the roots cool and the water steady, not to give up on the sun.

Check your lease and building rules first, covered in [rental balcony garden rules](/blog/balcony-garden-lease-and-building-rules/). Then check the [balcony weight limit](/blog/balcony-weight-limits-for-containers/), because the fixes below mean bigger, wetter pots. A fire escape is never the place for any of this, because it is an emergency exit and blocking it breaks fire code in most cities.

## Why is a city balcony hotter than the forecast?

A city balcony is hotter than the forecast because the forecast is measured in the shade over grass, and your balcony is neither. The [EPA's heat island page](https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/learn-about-heat-island-effects) says daytime temperatures in urban areas run about 1 to 7 degrees F higher than outlying areas, and nights about 2 to 5 degrees higher. Buildings and pavement soak up the sun's heat and give it back after dark.

A south or west balcony adds its own layer. The brick or concrete wall behind the pots heats up all afternoon and radiates onto the plants. A west exposure gets its sun at the hottest hours of the day, and wind dries the pots faster on the same afternoons.

The pots are the real problem. [North Carolina State Extension](https://swain.ces.ncsu.edu/news/hot-pots-what/) says the mix in a dark container in full sun runs 20 to 30 degrees F above the air temperature. On a 90-degree day that means roots sitting above 100, and the same page says five hours at that level, or 30 minutes at 113 degrees, leaves roots severely injured.

## Which vegetables can take a hot balcony?

The warm-season crops take a hot balcony best, and the cool-season greens do not belong there in summer. In the Happy Seeds kit that means tomato, pepper, eggplant, cucumber and melon are the summer balcony packets, and kale, spinach, carrot, beet and turnip are for spring and fall. Even the heat lovers pause in a heat wave and resume when the nights cool.

| Crop | What the heat does | The threshold (source) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tomato | Drops blossoms, ripens unevenly, no red above 85 F | Days above 85 F and nights above 70 F ([Minnesota Extension](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/how-excessive-heat-affects-vegetable-garden)) |
| Cucumber and melon | Mostly male flowers, bees slow down, misshapen fruit | Days above 90 F and nights above 70 F (Minnesota Extension) |
| Pepper and eggplant | Flowers drop, exposed fruit sunscalds | Same heat waves as tomato; thresholds in the [peppers and eggplant guide](/blog/peppers-and-eggplant-on-a-balcony/) |
| Kale, spinach, beet, turnip, carrot | Bolt, stall, or lose flavor | Some cool-season crops, carrots among them, turn bitter above 60 F ([Illinois Extension](https://extension.illinois.edu/blogs/over-garden-fence/2025-07-25-extreme-temperatures-impact-vegetable-gardens)) |

Those thresholds are air temperatures, measured in the shade. A south balcony against brick passes them earlier and more often than the forecast suggests.

## How do you keep pot roots cool on a hot balcony?

Keep roots cool by changing the pot, not the plant: a light-colored container, or a dark one slipped inside a bigger light one, mulched and grouped. North Carolina State's advice is to manage the temperature of the mix, with container color and sun exposure as two of its levers. A black nursery pot is the worst case; a pale pot, or a dark pot inside a bigger light one with an air gap, runs much cooler.

Grouping helps for free. [Clemson Extension](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/container-vegetable-gardening/) says to group containers so the foliage makes a canopy that shades the mix. It also says to lift pots onto a pallet or platform on a hot patio, and it warns about heat reflected off pavement, meaning the balcony floor and wall.

Mulch the surface of every pot. [Penn State Extension](https://extension.psu.edu/heat-proofing-your-vegetable-garden) says straw, other organic matter or unprinted cardboard holds moisture and lowers the soil temperature slightly; black plastic does the opposite. A layer of straw or shredded leaves on top of each pot is enough, and Clemson notes that clay pots dry faster and need watering more often.

| Fix | What it does | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Light-colored pot, or dark pot inside a bigger light one | Keeps the mix well under the 100 F root-injury zone | Low; the outer pot can be any pale bucket with drainage holes |
| Pots grouped, leaves touching | Foliage shades the mix; less wind on each pot | Free |
| Pots on a pallet or pot feet | Air under the pot; no contact with the hot floor | Free to low |
| Straw or leaf mulch on top | Holds water, cools the surface | Low |
| 30 to 50 percent shade cloth on the sunny side | Cuts the afternoon load on leaves and mix | Low to moderate |
| Water every morning, twice on the worst days | A wet mix stays cooler than a dry one | Free |

## How much shade does a hot balcony need, and when?

A hot balcony needs 30 to 50 percent shade cloth on its sunny side during the afternoon, and no shade at all on ordinary days. Penn State Extension recommends 30 to 50 percent cloth for most vegetables and says it must not touch the plants, because heat from the cloth can burn leaves. Illinois Extension suggests a 30 percent black cloth, which cools susceptible crops while leaving enough light for them to keep developing.

On a balcony the cloth hangs from the railing or a frame on the west or south side, not over the top, so the morning sun still reaches the plants. An old bedsheet clipped to the railing is the improvised version for a two-day heat wave, though it blocks more light than cloth. Tie it well against wind, and never fix anything to the building that your lease or house rules forbid.

Take the cloth down when the heat wave passes. Tomatoes, peppers and eggplant want their full sun back, and a shaded balcony in a normal week grows a leafy plant with little fruit.

## How often do you water pots in a heat wave?

Water every morning in a heat wave, and twice a day on hot, dry or windy days, until water runs out the drainage holes. Clemson Extension says to check containers at least once a day and twice on those days, and that daily or twice-daily watering is normal for vegetables in pots. Penn State adds the timing: water early, before the heat arrives, and a well-watered mix stays cooler than a dry one.

Drainage matters as much as water, because Penn State warns that waterlogged roots are especially damaged in warm weather. The everyday watering routine for pots, saucers and drips is in [the best vegetables for a balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/).

Go easy on fertilizer in a heat wave, and feed on the cooler weeks instead. North Carolina State notes that slow-release fertilizer starts releasing at around 70 to 80 degrees F in the mix and releases faster in a hotter pot. Minnesota Extension adds that too much nitrogen gives a lush plant with no fruit.

## What about the greens in summer?

Grow the cool-season five in spring and again for fall, and give the summer balcony to the fruiting crops. Bloomsdale spinach is sown 4 to 6 weeks before the last spring frost and again 6 to 8 weeks before the first fall frost.

Red Russian kale goes in 4 weeks before last frost and again 8 to 10 weeks before first frost. Find your dates on the [state planting calendars](/planting/) and confirm them with the county extension office.

A hot balcony is good news for the fall round. The wall that cooks pots in July holds warmth in October, so a south balcony often carries kale and spinach weeks past the first light frost. The site's guide to [fall vegetables to plant in late summer](/blog/fall-vegetables-to-plant-in-late-summer/) has the timing, and the shade cloth from August does double duty over the seedlings until the heat breaks.

If you want greens in July anyway, an east-facing window indoors is a better bet than the balcony. The series post on [windowsill vegetables from seed](/blog/windowsill-vegetables-from-seed/) covers which of the five manage there.
