The short version is that seeds do not expire the way milk does. A seed is a living embryo in a dry coat.

It loses the ability to sprout slowly, over years, at a pace set by the crop and by how it was stored. The date on the packet is a freshness mark that tells you where that decline started.

That matters when a half-used packet turns up in a drawer, and it matters when you buy. Below is what the date means, how many years each vegetable seed lasts, a ten-seed test for any packet, and the storage that stretches the answer.

## What does the packed-for date on a seed packet mean?

The packed-for date is the year the seed was packed for sale after passing a germination test, not the date it stops being usable. Seed labels are governed state by state, and Minnesota's rules are a clear example.

The Minnesota Department of Agriculture's [guide to home garden seed labels](https://www.mda.state.mn.us/sites/default/files/docs/2024-03/Seed-Label-Home-Gardeners-2023-web_0.pdf) defines it as the year the seed was packed for sale with germination above the legal standard. For selling purposes, that date expires on December 31 of the year stated.

Under those rules an optional sell-by date works the same way and may not run more than twelve months past the germination test. Most states follow similar seed laws, so a packet dated for this year was tested within roughly the last year and met the minimum. A packet from three years ago was fresh then, and has been declining since.

The post on [how to read a seed packet](/blog/how-to-read-a-seed-packet/) walks through the rest of the label. The piece that matters most here is the germination rate, explained in [what a germination rate means](/blog/what-germination-rate-means/), because it tells you how strong the seed was at the start.

## How long do vegetable seeds last?

Most vegetable seeds last two to five years in cool, dry storage, and the range by crop is wide. University of Illinois Extension's [seed viability sheet](https://extension.illinois.edu/sites/default/files/infosheet_seed_viability.pdf) gives approximate seed life in years.

K-State Research and Extension's note on [how long garden seed can be kept](https://www.k-state.edu/news/wild-west-district/articles/2023/03/how-long-can-garden-seed-be-kept-by-ron-honig.html) gives similar figures for most crops. The table shows the ten crops in a typical beginner kit, plus a few short-lived ones for contrast.

| Crop | Approximate seed life (Illinois) | K-State range, where given |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cucumber | 5 years | 4 to 5 years (cucurbits) |
| Melon (muskmelon) | 5 years | 4 to 5 years (melons) |
| Tomato | 4 years | 4 years |
| Eggplant | 4 years | not listed |
| Kale | 4 years | 4 to 5 years (crucifers) |
| Beet | 4 years | 2 to 3 years |
| Turnip | 4 years | not listed |
| Carrot | 3 years | 2 to 3 years |
| Spinach | 3 years | 2 to 3 years |
| Pepper | 2 years | 2 years |
| Sweet corn | 2 years | 2 to 3 years |
| Onion | 1 year | 1 year |
| Parsnip | 1 year | 1 year |

These are averages under ordinary storage, which is why the two sources disagree a little, most visibly on beets. University of Vermont Extension's January 2026 note on [whether old seeds can still grow](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/can-old-seeds-still-grow) sums it up. Most seeds stay viable for an additional year or two, some longer, and the older the seed, the lower the germination rate.

Two patterns are worth remembering. Pepper is the one warm-season crop in the kit with a short life, so old pepper seed is the first to disappoint. Onion, parsnip and parsley are the true one-year seeds, so a leftover packet of any of them is the first to test and the first to replace.

## How do you tell if old seeds are still good?

The reliable way is a ten-seed germination test on a damp paper towel, which takes one to three weeks depending on the crop and costs nothing. Illinois Extension's method is to lay ten seeds on a damp paper towel, fold it over and slide it into a plastic bag.

Label the bag with the crop, the date and the days to germination from the packet. Keep it somewhere warm; it does not need light.

After the packet's days-to-germination have passed, open the towel and count the seeds that have begun to grow. The sheet's rule of thumb is in the table.

| Seeds sprouted out of 10 | What it means | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | Germination is strong | Sow as the packet directs |
| 7 to 9 | Germination has slipped | Sow extra seeds per spot and thin later |
| 6 or fewer | Germination is poor | Buy fresh seed |

Vermont Extension uses a similar test and a slightly looser cutoff: if fewer than half sprout, consider buying new seed. Either way, a test tells you more than the date does, because it measures the packet in your hand after the storage it actually had.

One more sign from the Illinois sheet: seeds that sprouted on their own in storage, because the packet got damp, will not grow when planted. Dry seed that looks unchanged is the only kind worth testing.

## How should you store seeds so they last?

Store seeds dry, sealed and cold, and they can outlast the figures in the table. K-State's advice is a zip-locked bag or plastic jar kept around 40 to 50 degrees F, and it calls the refrigerator a better choice than the freezer.

A home refrigerator runs a little cooler than that, which is fine as long as the seed is sealed and dry. Illinois Extension sets the mark at below 45 degrees F with low humidity, and notes that the drier the seed goes into storage, the longer it keeps.

The practical version for most homes is a sealed jar in the refrigerator, with the original packets inside so the crop, variety and germination rate stay with the seed. Let the jar come to room temperature before opening it, so moisture does not condense on cold seed. A kitchen drawer beside the stove is the worst common spot, because it is warm and swings with every meal.

If the refrigerator is full, a closet on an interior wall is the next best thing. The enemies are heat, moisture and the daily swings between them.

## How does this change what you buy?

Buy seed with a printed germination rate and a current packed-for date, and buy packet sizes you will use within the crop's seed life. A packet of pepper seed has roughly two good years, so a small packet is the right size. A packet of cucumber or tomato seed can stretch across four or five seasons if it is stored well.
