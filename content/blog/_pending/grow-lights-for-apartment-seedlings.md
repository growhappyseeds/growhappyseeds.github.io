This guide is for the apartment gardener with no balcony, and for the balcony gardener who starts seeds inside before the weather allows them out. In both apartments the question is the same: is the window enough, or do you need a lamp?

Here is how to tell, and what a simple setup looks like when the answer is a lamp. For what each window direction can grow on its own, read [which window direction is best for growing vegetables indoors](/blog/best-window-direction-for-growing-vegetables/).

## Do you need a grow light to start seeds in an apartment?

You need a grow light to start seeds in an apartment unless you have an unobstructed south-facing window and are growing only quick cool-season greens. University of Minnesota Extension's guide to [lighting for indoor plants and starting seeds](https://extension.umn.edu/planting-and-growing-guides/lighting-indoor-plants) says a high-light spot may start seeds without artificial lighting. It adds that seeds that need more time indoors, such as tomatoes and peppers, may become leggy without extra light.

That is the whole answer in one sentence. Time indoors is what decides it, not the crop's size or its sun needs later on. Kale sown in a sill pot at a south window grows fine; a tomato seedling at the same window for seven weeks is a stretched, pale thing that falls over.

A North Dakota State University Extension agent describes the usual result in [Grow lights for seed starting](https://www.ag.ndsu.edu/news/columns/dakota-gardener/dakota-gardener-grow-lights-for-seed-starting). After many tries at starting seeds in south-facing windows, her seedlings were tall, thin and floppy, and she switched to lights. A city window with a building across the street gets even fewer hours of direct sun.

## Which seeds can start in a window, and which need a lamp?

Cool-season greens and roots can start in a bright window; the warm-season crops that spend six to eight weeks indoors need a lamp. The table sorts the ten varieties in the [Happy Seeds Veggie Garden Seed Kit](/) by how long each one sits indoors, using the sowing timing for each variety.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Kale, spinach, beet, turnip | None required: direct sow in a sill pot or balcony planter | Works for baby greens | Faster, fuller leaves in winter |
| Carrot | None required: direct sow in a deep pot | Works, slowly | Works |
| Tomato (Floradade) | 6 to 8 weeks | Leggy | Short, stocky transplants |
| Pepper (Anaheim Chili), eggplant (Black Beauty) | 8 weeks | Leggy | Short, stocky transplants |
| Cucumber (Marketmore 76) | None: direct sow outdoors once the soil is warm | Not needed | Not needed |
| Melon (Honeydew Orange) | None, or 3 weeks indoors before transplanting | Workable for the short start | Works |

The honest version of the kit for an apartment runs like this. The five cool-season packets grow in a sunny window or a balcony planter, with or without a lamp. The warm-season five want a big container and real sun.

If that container is going on a balcony, check your lease or building rules and the balcony's weight limit first. A no-balcony reader starts them indoors and needs a balcony, a roof the building allows, or a community plot later. The box holds seeds, the guide and the box itself; the lamp, pots and soil are your shopping list.

If you have no balcony and no plans for an outdoor spot, you can skip the lamp entirely. Sow the greens at the window and leave the tomato and pepper packets sealed for a future season. ## What a basic grow light setup looks like in a small apartment

A basic setup is one LED shop light, two short chains or a shelf to hang it from, a plug-in timer and a tray that fits underneath. University of New Hampshire Extension's fact sheet on [growing seedlings under lights](https://extension.unh.edu/resource/growing-seedlings-under-lights-fact-sheet) says most seedlings can be grown to transplant stage under four-bulb T8 fluorescent shop lights. A bright white LED shop light of similar length generally does the same job, runs cooler and weighs less, which matters on a rental shelf.

Hang the light from a bookshelf, a wire kitchen rack or a closet rod with the chains that come with the fixture. Put the tray on the shelf below and adjust the chain, not the tray, as the seedlings grow. Books under the tray work when the fixture cannot move.

University of Vermont Extension's [tips for choosing grow lights](https://www.uvm.edu/extension/news/tips-choosing-grow-lights) say a bulb rated near 6500K is ideal, because that matches natural daylight. The same page suggests a color rendering index above 85, and says seedlings need 16 to 18 hours of light a day at 65 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit. Color temperature is printed on most shop-light boxes and CRI on many, and most hardware stores carry a fixture that meets them.

Skip the dim purple lights sold as seedling kits. The UNH fact sheet measured one cheap purple LED that would have needed 34 hours of running time a day to give seedlings enough light, which is impossible. Incandescent bulbs are worse: NDSU Extension says they do not work well for starting seeds and generate a lot of heat.

## How far above the seedlings, and how many hours a day?

Keep fluorescent tubes a few inches above the seedlings, LEDs 8 to 12 inches above, and run the light 14 to 16 hours a day on a timer. Iowa State University Extension's answer to [how do I set up supplemental lights for my seedlings](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/how-do-i-set-supplemental-lights-my-seedlings) gives the distances. It says 4 to 6 inches for most fluorescent fixtures and about 8 to 12 inches for LEDs.

The same page sets the timer at 14 to 16 hours a day. University of Minnesota Extension says 16 to 18 hours for seedlings, so anywhere in that range is fine.

| Setting | Extension guidance | What to do in the apartment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hours per day | 14 to 16 (Iowa State); 16 to 18 (Minnesota) | Timer on at 6 a.m., off at 9 or 10 p.m. |
| Fluorescent distance | 4 to 6 inches (Iowa State) | Raise the chain every few days |
| LED distance | 8 to 12 inches (Iowa State) | Start at 8 inches; lift if leaves bleach |
| Darkness | Plants require periods of darkness (Vermont) | Never run the light all night |
| Temperature | 65 to 75 degrees Fahrenheit (Vermont) | Most heated apartments already sit there; keep the tray off a hot radiator |

The timer is the part people skip, and the part that makes the system work in an apartment. Without it, the light goes on when you remember and off at bedtime, so the seedlings get a short, uneven day that changes with your schedule. A mechanical plug-in timer from the hardware store solves it.

Watch the seedlings, not the ruler. If they lean and stretch, the light is too high or too weak; if the newest leaves look washed out, it is too close.

Iowa State also recommends air circulation around the seedlings to carry off the fixture's heat. A small fan on low does that, and gentle air movement is widely credited with sturdier stems.

## Timing: when the lamp goes on, and when it goes off for the year

The lamp goes on the day the first seedlings break the surface, and it goes off for the year once the last transplant has moved outside. Most vegetable seeds do not need light to sprout, so the tray can sit in a warm spot until then. From the first green loop of stem onward, every hour without strong light is an hour of stretching.

Count backward from your last spring frost to set the start date, and confirm that date with your county extension office before you sow. The [planting guide](/planting/) lists frost windows by state, and [when to start seeds indoors](/blog/when-to-start-seeds-indoors/) covers the crop-by-crop schedule.

Where the last frost comes in late April or May, one light running from late February to mid May covers the tomato, pepper and eggplant packets. Shift that window to match your own frost date.
