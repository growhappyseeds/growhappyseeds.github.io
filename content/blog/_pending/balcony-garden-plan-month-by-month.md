This plan is for the apartment with a balcony, terrace or roof deck the building lets you use, and for the reader who starts seedlings indoors first. The constraint is space and the calendar: one planter has to do two jobs in a single short outdoor season, and seeds sown a month late or early waste it. The answer is to plan the year around two dates and sow everything relative to them.

## What two dates decide a balcony garden calendar?

Your average last spring frost and average first fall frost decide every sowing date on a balcony. Every packet is sown a set number of weeks before or after one of those two dates, so once you know them the year fills itself in. Find yours on the [state planting pages](/planting/) and confirm them with your county extension office.

This article uses New York City as the worked example. The New York Botanical Garden puts Central Park's average last spring freeze at [April 1 to 15](https://libanswers.nybg.org/faq/222609), based on NOAA climate normals. Across the tri-state area it can be as late as April 30.

Its average first fall frost is [November 20](https://libanswers.nybg.org/faq/377020), with a range from mid-October to late December.

Pots freeze sooner than garden soil and a balcony is more exposed, so take the later end of the spring range and the earlier end of the fall range. If you garden somewhere else, shift every month below by the gap between your frost dates and New York's.

A Chicago balcony runs roughly three to four weeks later in spring and about a month earlier in fall. A Houston balcony runs so early that summer, not winter, is the dead season.

## How does the balcony year look month by month?

The year has four working seasons on a balcony. They are indoor starts in late winter, cool sowings in early spring, the warm-season move outside after the frost, and a second cool sowing in late summer.

The table uses the Happy Seeds sowing windows and the New York dates above. Dates are approximate and shift with your own frost dates.

| Month (NYC) | Indoors | On the balcony |
| --- | --- | --- |
| January | Order seeds, test old ones, clean pots. | Nothing to sow. Measure sun hours and check the weight limit. |
| February | Mid-month: sow peppers and eggplant, 8 weeks before the last frost. Late month: sow tomatoes. | Still nothing outside. |
| March | Pot up seedlings as they outgrow cells. | Early to mid-month: sow spinach and kale in planters. Late month: beets, turnips, carrots. |
| April | Harden off the warm-season seedlings in the last two weeks. | Harvest baby spinach and kale. Direct sow cucumbers at month end if the mix is warm. |
| May | Clear the shelves. | Move tomatoes into their big pots 1 to 2 weeks after the last frost, peppers and eggplant 2 weeks after. |
| June | Nothing. | Pull bolting spinach. Stake tomatoes, train cucumbers up the trellis. |
| July | Nothing. | Harvest cucumbers and the first tomatoes. Too hot to sow greens. |
| August | Nothing. | Late month: sow carrots for fall, 10 to 12 weeks before the first frost. |
| September | Nothing. | Sow kale, beets and turnips by mid-month; spinach late in the month. |
| October | Nothing. | Harvest the last tomatoes and peppers. Thin fall greens. |
| November | Nothing. | Warm-season plants come down at the first frost. Protect the hardy greens. |
| December | Plan next year. | Harvest kale and spinach slowly if protected. |

The spring column for the cool-season packets comes straight from the Happy Seeds sowing windows for each variety. Spinach is sown 4 to 6 weeks before the last frost, kale 4 weeks before, beets and turnips 2 to 4 weeks before, and carrots 2 to 3 weeks before. Their fall windows are counted back from the first frost instead.

## Which seeds start indoors, and when?

Peppers and eggplant start indoors 8 weeks before the last frost, tomatoes 6 to 8 weeks before, and everything else in the box is sown straight into the balcony planter. In the New York example that puts peppers and eggplant in mid-February and tomatoes at the end of February. Starting earlier usually gives you a leggier, root-bound plant on the shelf, not a better one outside.

Seed germinates fastest when the mix is warm. The University of California Master Gardener program's [soil temperature chart](https://ucanr.edu/program/uc-master-gardener-program/seed-germination-temperature-and-timing) puts the best range for tomato germination at 65 to 85 degrees F. Seeds take roughly one to two weeks across that range and over 40 days at 50 degrees F.

A cold windowsill in February is often the problem, so start the trays somewhere warmer and move them to the light once they are up.

The series has already covered [where to start seeds in a small apartment](/blog/starting-seeds-in-a-small-apartment/) and [whether you need a grow light](/blog/grow-lights-for-apartment-seedlings/), so this plan only assigns the dates.

Cucumber and melon are the exception among the warm-season packets. Cucumber is direct sown 1 to 2 weeks after the frost once the mix is warm. Melon is direct sown 2 weeks after it, or started indoors only 3 weeks before it goes out.

## When does everything move outside?

Cool-season sowings go into the planters 2 to 6 weeks before the last frost, and warm-season transplants go out 1 to 2 weeks after it. That gap of three weeks to two months is the whole trick of a balcony year: the same planter grows spinach in April and holds a cucumber by June. In New York the greens go out in March and the tomatoes in early May.

The move itself needs a week or two of hardening off, which the [start indoors, finish on the balcony](/blog/start-indoors-move-to-balcony/) article walks through day by day. Build those days into the plan, so a tomato sown in late February is being carried in and out during the last two weeks of April.

Before the first pot goes out, the balcony itself needs two checks. Read the lease and building rules on planters, railings and drips, covered in the [rental balcony rules](/blog/balcony-garden-lease-and-building-rules/) article. Then find out the weight limit, covered in [how heavy is a wet planter](/blog/balcony-weight-limits-for-containers/).

A plan that fits the calendar but breaks a house rule ends in June.

## How do you keep greens coming all season?

Sow small amounts of greens every week or two during each cool window, rather than the whole packet at once. The University of Connecticut's home garden program suggests [smaller harvests maturing every week or two](https://homegarden.cahnr.uconn.edu/factsheets/succession-planting). The University of Maryland Extension's [succession planting guide](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/planting-vegetables-succession) suggests planting a portion of the space every two weeks.

On a balcony that means a few rows of spinach in the same planter, two weeks apart, through March and April.

| Cool-season packet | Spring window (NYC) | Fall window (NYC) | Summer? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spinach | Early March to mid-April | Late September to early October | No, bolts |
| Kale | Mid-March to April | Mid to late September | Slows, leaves toughen |
| Beets | Late March to April | Mid to late September | Poor in heat |
| Turnips | Late March to April | Mid to late September | Poor in heat |
| Carrots | Late March to April | Late August to early September | Slow, roots stay small |

Summer is the gap. Spinach seed germinates poorly in mix above about 75 degrees F by the UC chart, and the other cool-season packets bolt or stall in balcony heat. July is for harvesting the warm-season crops, not sowing greens.

The fall window is the second chance, and on many balconies the better one.

## When does the balcony year end?

The warm-season plants end at the first frost, and the hardy greens carry on for weeks after it if the pots are protected. In New York that means tomatoes, peppers, eggplant and cucumbers come down in mid-November at the latest, and often earlier on a windy high floor. Kale and spinach sown in September are the plants still standing in December.

Picking green tomatoes before the frost and finishing them indoors is a fair ending for the season. The [fall and winter greens on a balcony](/blog/fall-and-winter-balcony-greens/) article covers which greens survive which temperatures and how to wrap the pots.

December is for emptying the dead pots, storing the mix dry, and writing down what each month actually looked like on your balcony. Next year's plan is this year's notes.
