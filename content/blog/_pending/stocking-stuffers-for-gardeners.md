A stocking stuffer for a gardener has to pass two tests: it must fit in a stocking, and it must still be useful in spring. Most gift-shop garden items fail the second one. The ideas below are small, under $25 in most cases, and chosen because a gardener would buy them for themselves.

They are sorted by what they do rather than by price, because a beginner and a 30-year gardener want different things from the same stocking. For bigger gifts and full kits, the [seed gift guide](/blog/best-seed-gifts/) and [gifts for new gardeners](/blog/gifts-for-new-gardeners/) cover the rest of the list.

## What makes a good stocking stuffer for a gardener?

A good gardening stocking stuffer is small, durable and seasonless: it keeps in a drawer through winter and goes to work the first warm weekend. Durability matters more than looks, since the item will see dirt, water and sun. The cheapest versions of most garden tools are the ones that break, so one well-made thing beats three flimsy ones.

Usefulness is the second filter. A gardener already owns a trowel, so a second plastic one is clutter, while a soil thermometer or a tool-cleaning block fills a gap most people never buy for themselves. When in doubt, pick the consumable or the upgrade, not the duplicate.

## Seeds and seed-starting gifts

Seeds are the classic stocking stuffer for gardeners because packets are flat and light. Most vegetable seeds keep for several years in a cool, dry place. The one risk is choosing varieties they will not plant, which is why a kit with named varieties beats a random packet for anyone whose tastes you do not know.

1. **A complete vegetable seed kit.** Happy Seeds is the complete first vegetable garden in one box: ten varieties, 1,000+ lab-tested non-GMO seeds, germination rate printed on every packet, a 24-page grow guide, $24.99. The slide-out keepsake box sits beside the stocking or under the tree. The five cool-season packets can be sown in a pot on a sunny windowsill, while the warm-season five wait for a big container and spring sun.
2. **A single packet of something they love.** A gardener who grows the same tomato every year will plant a packet of it in a stocking without a second thought. Read the packet before buying; the post on [how to read a seed packet](/blog/how-to-read-a-seed-packet/) explains the packed-for date and the germination rate to look for.
3. **Seed-starting labels.** Wooden or plastic labels and a pencil solve the problem every seed starter has in March: six trays of identical green sprouts. A pack is a few dollars and disappears into any stocking.
4. **A soil thermometer.** The single most useful cheap tool for a seed starter, since warm-season seeds such as tomato, pepper, cucumber and melon will not germinate in cold soil. It tells the gardener when the bed is ready instead of guessing from the calendar.

## Hand tools that fit in a stocking

Small cutting and tying tools are the stocking stuffers gardeners reach for most, because they wear out or go missing every season. Look for metal where it matters and a brand the recipient would recognize, and skip anything with a painted floral handle.

5. **Bypass pruners.** A good pair is the top of the budget but the gift that lasts longest; bypass blades cut cleanly where anvil blades crush soft stems.
6. **Garden snips.** Lighter than pruners, for harvesting greens, deadheading and cutting twine; they fit a stocking toe.
7. **A hori-hori or a hand weeder.** A narrow, pointed blade for weeding, dividing and planting bulbs, and a tool many gardeners never buy themselves.
8. **A tool-cleaning block or oil.** A rust eraser and a small bottle of tool oil keep pruners sharp and closing smoothly. It is a gift for someone who already has good tools.

## Gloves, labels, twine and the things that run out

Consumables are safe stocking stuffers because no gardener has too much twine, too many labels or too many gloves. These are the gifts to add if the stocking has room left after one good tool.

9. **Gloves.** Nitrile-coated gloves for seed starting and weeding, or leather palms for pruning; buy the recipient's size, not one-size.
10. **Metal plant labels.** Zinc or copper labels marked in pencil or paint pen last for seasons, where marker on a plastic tag fades within one.
11. **Jute twine.** A ball of natural twine ties tomatoes, cucumbers and peas to their supports and composts at the end of the season.
12. **Plant ties or clips.** Soft ties or small trellis clips are the thing a balcony tomato grower runs out of in August.

## Gifts for the gardener who has everything

For the gardener with a full tool shed, the best stocking stuffers add knowledge or comfort rather than another tool. These three suit the person who already grows well and would like to grow better.

13. **A pocket garden journal.** A small notebook for sowing dates, frost dates and what worked; gardeners who keep one plant better the next year. Add a note with their last frost date from the [state planting calendars](/planting/), and remind them to confirm it with their county extension office.
14. **A hand lens.** A 10x loupe shows aphids, seed structure and leaf disease a phone camera misses, and it costs less than a bag of potting mix.
15. **Gardener's hand soap or salve.** Pumice or coffee-ground soap and a plain hand salve are the one scented gift that gets used, because garden hands need it.

## What to skip in a gardener's stocking

Skip novelty tool sets, decorative items and anything that assumes a yard. Painted-handle tool sets bend on the first root; ceramic frogs and signs do nothing; a bulb planter is useless to someone who gardens in pots on a balcony.

Be careful with seeds for the wrong climate. Melons and peppers for a gardener in a short northern season, or spinach for a Florida summer, will sit in the drawer. When you do not know what they grow, a mixed kit with cool-season and warm-season crops covers both seasons.

Finally, do not buy the gift-shop "garden in a can" with no variety name and no seed count. A gardener will read the label, and a label that says only "tomato" tells them nothing. Named varieties, a seed count and a printed germination rate are the three lines that separate a seed gift from a novelty.
