This guide is for the apartment with a small balcony: a few feet of floor, a railing, and enough sun for one serious plant. It is not for the windowsill-only apartment, where a tomato can be started but never finished. The constraint is sun, pot size, wind and weight, and one plant in one big pot answers all four.

Two things to settle before you buy potting mix. Read your lease and your building's rules on balcony planters, covered in the series post on [rental balcony garden rules](/blog/balcony-garden-lease-and-building-rules/). Then weigh what you are planning: a 5-gallon pot full of wet mix can run 40 pounds or more, and the [balcony weight limits post](/blog/balcony-weight-limits-for-containers/) explains how to check yours.

## Can a tomato grow on a small balcony?

A tomato grows on a small balcony when the plant gets at least six hours of direct sun, and that is the first thing to confirm. [Clemson Extension's guide to tomatoes in containers](https://hgic.clemson.edu/tomatoes-in-containers/) puts six hours as the minimum, and [Virginia Tech's container vegetable guide](https://pubs.ext.vt.edu/426/426-336.html) adds that fruiting vegetables do better with eight to ten. A south, southwest or west balcony usually gets there; an east one is borderline, and a north one is not a tomato balcony.

Count the hours on a clear day in late spring, not in winter, because the sun is higher and neighboring buildings block less of it. The site's guide to [the best vegetables for a balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/) covers how to measure your exposure and what a north-facing balcony can still grow instead.

If the hours are there, one plant is the right number. A tomato in full fruit is a bush 3 to 4 feet tall and about 2 feet across, which fills the corner of a small balcony on its own.

## What pot and mix does a balcony tomato need?

A balcony tomato needs a pot of 5 gallons or more with drainage holes, and a lightweight potting mix rather than garden soil. [Iowa State Extension](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/growing-tomatoes-containers) gives 4 gallons, roughly a 12 to 14 inch pot, as the minimum for a standard tomato, and Virginia Tech lists 5 gallons for one plant. The Floradade tomato in the Happy Seeds kit is listed for a 5-gallon or larger container.

| Choice | Small-balcony guidance | Why it matters on a balcony |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pot size | 5 gallons or more, one plant per pot | A bigger pot dries out more slowly in wind and holds the plant up |
| Material | Plastic, fabric or glazed; terracotta is heavier and dries fastest | Weight counts against your balcony limit |
| Mix | Bagged potting mix, never soil dug from a park or planter | Garden soil compacts in a pot and may carry pests inside |
| Drainage | Holes in the base plus a saucer or tray | Drips onto the neighbor below are the most common complaint |
| Support | A stake or cage set at planting time | Setting it later damages the roots |

Iowa State recommends a soilless mix that drains well without drying too fast, and notes that many bagged mixes already contain slow-release fertilizer. If yours does, you can wait until midsummer to start feeding. Put the stake or cage in the pot the day you plant, as Virginia Tech advises, so the roots are never disturbed.

Keep the pot on a saucer and the saucer off the floor on a couple of bricks or pot feet. That stops water pooling against the slab and keeps drips off the balcony below, which is often the rule that gets planters banned.

## When do you start tomato seeds for a balcony?

Start tomato seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your last spring frost and move the plant to the balcony 1 to 2 weeks after that date. Those are the kit's own timings for Floradade, and they put sowing in late winter for most of the country. Find your last-frost window on the [state planting calendars](/planting/) and confirm it with your county extension office, because a city block can be warmer or colder than the county average.

The indoor weeks are the same as for any apartment seedling: a bright south window or a lamp on a timer. The series post on [grow lights for apartment seedlings](/blog/grow-lights-for-apartment-seedlings/) covers the lamp. Sow two or three seeds in a 3 or 4 inch pot, keep the strongest, and pot it up once when its roots fill that pot. Aim for a stocky plant 6 to 10 inches tall by moving day.

Do not rush the move. Tomatoes sulk below 50 degrees F at night, and a plant set out into a cold week stalls for longer than it would have waited indoors. Harden it off over a week or two first, following the series post on [hardening off seedlings on a balcony](/blog/start-indoors-move-to-balcony/).

## How do you keep a balcony tomato watered?

Water a balcony tomato once a day in summer, and twice on hot or windy days, until water runs out the drainage holes. Virginia Tech says to check containers at least once a day and twice on hot, dry or windy days, and Clemson expects daily watering at the height of summer. A balcony is windier and hotter than a garden bed, so the pot dries faster than any garden guide assumes.

Iowa State warns never to let the mix dry out completely, because the plant responds by dropping flowers and fruit. Push a finger an inch into the mix each morning; if it is dry, water. If you travel, a second saucer that holds a reservoir, or a neighbor with a key, is part of the plan.

Feed from midsummer, or from planting if the mix has no fertilizer in it. Iowa State suggests a water-soluble fertilizer every week or two. Virginia Tech says a mix with fertilizer added carries the plant for eight to ten weeks.

## Why do balcony tomato flowers drop, and how do you get fruit?

Balcony tomato flowers drop mostly because of heat, and the fix is afternoon shade and steady water rather than anything sprayed on the flowers. [Maryland Extension's note on fruit set](https://extension.umd.edu/agnr.umd.edu/sites/extension.umd.edu/files/2021-06/HelpingFruitSetTomatoes_2015_08.pdf) says that several days above 86 degrees F, or nights above 70, make the plant abort flowers. [Rutgers](https://plant-pest-advisory.rutgers.edu/high-temperatures-and-effect-on-tomato-pollination-and-fruit-set/) puts the fruit-set drop at days over 90 and nights over 70.

A south or west balcony against a brick wall runs well above the forecast, which is the heat-island problem. During a heat wave, rig a piece of shade cloth or an old sheet on the sunny side for the afternoon, keep the pot watered, and wait. Maryland Extension found that shading once fruit set begins raised yields in its trials, and the next flush of flowers usually sets when the nights cool.

Pollination itself is rarely the problem outdoors. Tomato flowers are self-pollinating and shed pollen when the plant moves, which a balcony breeze does all day. If flowers open and fall in mild, calm weather, tap the stake or shake each cluster gently around midday a few times a week.

| Symptom | Likely cause on a balcony | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Flowers yellow and drop in a hot week | Days above 86 to 90 F or nights above 70 | Afternoon shade, water daily, wait for cooler nights |
| Flowers drop after the mix went bone dry | Drought stress | Water more deeply and more often; add a reservoir saucer |
| Tall, pale plant with few flowers | Not enough sun | Move the pot to the sunniest spot or accept a smaller crop |
| Fruit stays orange-red, not deep red | Ripening above 90 F | Pick at first blush and finish ripening indoors |

The last row comes from Iowa State, which notes that the red pigments in tomato fruit do not form well above 90 degrees F. A fruit picked when it first colors ripens fully on the counter.

## How long until you pick a balcony tomato?

Expect the first ripe tomato roughly 75 to 80 days after the plant goes out on the balcony, which is Floradade's figure from transplant. With a move in mid-May that means early August, and the plant keeps ripening fruit through late summer. A pot-grown tomato tends to ripen much of its crop over a few weeks, so plan to eat or share a lot of tomatoes at once. When the plant finishes, empty the pot into a bag for the building's compost or green bin, not down the balcony drain, and clean the pot for next spring.
