Tomatoes are the crop that makes a first garden feel like a garden, and the one that punishes a late or careless start. The whole route from seed to a ripe fruit takes four to five months, but only a few steps in it decide the outcome. This guide covers each one for a garden bed or raised bed, with Floradade, the tomato in the Happy Seeds kit, as the worked example.

## When do you start tomato seeds?

Start tomato seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your average last spring frost. That is the window Happy Seeds gives for Floradade, and it matches the [NC State Extension guide](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/growing-tomatoes-in-the-home-garden), which says six to ten weeks before the last frost. Find your date on the [state planting pages](/planting/) and confirm it with your county extension office.

The reason not to start earlier is the plant, not the calendar. A tomato held indoors for twelve weeks is tall, pale and root-bound, and a stockier six-week plant soon overtakes it outside. In most of the Northeast and Midwest, sowing falls in March; in the Deep South it falls in January or February.

Tomatoes are started indoors because they need warm soil and a long season, which is why [when to start seeds indoors](/blog/when-to-start-seeds-indoors/) lists them first.

## How do you germinate tomato seeds?

Tomato seeds germinate in warm, moist mix, ideally at 65 to 85 degrees F, and need no light until they sprout. The University of California Master Gardener program's [germination chart](https://ucanr.edu/program/uc-master-gardener-program/seed-germination-temperature-and-timing) puts emergence at about 6 to 8 days near the top of that range. At 59 degrees F it is about two weeks.

At 50 degrees F the same seed takes over 40 days, which is why a cold windowsill in March looks like a bad packet.

| Mix temperature | Days for tomato seeds to emerge (UC chart) |
| --- | --- |
| 50 degrees F | about 43 |
| 59 degrees F | about 14 |
| 68 degrees F | about 8 |
| 77 to 86 degrees F | about 6 |

Sow two seeds per cell or small pot, about a quarter inch deep, in a seed-starting mix rather than garden soil. Keep the mix damp and the tray somewhere warm, such as a seedling heat mat or a shelf near a warm appliance, and check it daily. Once the first sprout shows, move the tray into light the same day.

A packet with the germination rate printed on it tells you what to expect from those two seeds; [what germination rate means](/blog/what-germination-rate-means/) explains the number.

## How do you keep tomato seedlings stocky?

Tomato seedlings stay stocky with strong light, cool nights and a bigger pot once the roots fill the cell. Light is the usual failure: a window gives far less than the sun outside, so seedlings reach for it and fall over. A south window helps, and an inexpensive lamp a few inches above the leaves helps more; [leggy seedlings indoors](/blog/leggy-seedlings-indoors/) covers the fix in detail.

Thin to one seedling per cell by snipping the weaker one at the soil line. When the plant has two or three sets of true leaves, move it to a 3- or 4-inch pot and bury the stem up to the lowest leaves. That buried stem grows roots, and it is the first of two chances to plant a tomato deep.

Let the surface dry slightly between waterings, and run a fan on low or brush a hand over the tops each day to thicken the stems. Feed lightly with a diluted liquid fertilizer only after the true leaves appear.

## How do you move tomato seedlings outside?

Harden the seedlings off over a week or two, then plant them 1 to 2 weeks after the last frost, once the soil has warmed. The [University of Maryland Extension](https://www.extension.umd.edu/resource/growing-tomatoes-home-garden) says to transplant after all danger of frost is past and when the soil has warmed. It warns against setting the root ball into cold soil.

Frost injures the top growth and cold soil slows the roots.

Hardening off means a few hours of shade outside the first day, a little more sun each day, and nights indoors until the end. The [start indoors, finish on the balcony](/blog/start-indoors-move-to-balcony/) article gives a day-by-day schedule that works the same for a garden bed. Skip it and a day of full sun can scald the leaves white.

Plant deep. Maryland Extension's advice is to bury the main stem so only two or three sets of true leaves are exposed. A leggy plant can lie sideways in a shallow trench, so roots form along the buried stem.

NC State spaces plants 1 1/2 to 2 feet apart in the row with 3 to 4 feet between rows, and says to stake or cage shortly after planting.

## How do you support and prune a tomato plant?

Stake or cage every tomato the week it goes in, and prune only staked plants of the climbing kind. The two methods trade off: NC State says staking gives larger but fewer tomatoes than caging.

A stake is a 6-foot post driven about a foot deep a few inches from the stem. A cage is a wire cylinder about 2 feet across, pinned with stakes so summer storms do not tip it.

| Support | Pruning | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stake, tied loosely with cloth or twine | Climbing types: prune to 1 to 3 main stems, pinch other suckers weekly while small. Bush types: prune once, lightly, or not at all | Larger, earlier fruit; plants can be spaced closer |
| Wire cage about 2 feet across | None; push suckers back inside the cage | More, smaller fruit; less work |
| Sprawl on thick mulch | None | Most fruit in contact with the ground; more rot and slugs |

A sucker is the shoot that appears in the angle between the main stem and a leaf. Maryland Extension says to remove them weekly on staked plants, especially the ones at the base, by pinching with your fingers. That advice is for indeterminate varieties, the kind that keep climbing until frost.

Determinate or bush varieties stop at a set height and carry much of their fruit on side shoots, so heavy pruning cuts the crop. NC State says to prune those once, when the suckers are 2 to 4 inches long, or to cage them and leave them alone. Floradade is generally listed as a determinate variety, so on a stake it needs that one light pruning at most.

## How do you water and feed tomatoes?

Water deeply and evenly, about 1 1/2 inches a week once fruit is setting, and mulch to hold the moisture steady. NC State's figure is 1.5 inches a week from rain or irrigation during fruiting, soaking the soil 6 to 8 inches down at roughly weekly intervals. Most of a tomato's feeder roots sit in the top foot of soil, so a thick mulch of straw or composted leaves does double duty against weeds and drying.

Uneven water is what causes blossom end rot, the leathery black patch on the bottom of the fruit. [Purdue Extension](https://www.purdue.edu/hla/sites/yardandgarden/consistent-soil-moisture-helps-prevent-blossom-end-rot/) says the scar is a calcium shortage in the developing fruit, most often tied to extreme swings in soil moisture. No spray controls it, in Purdue's words, except perhaps the irrigation hose.

Watering during dry spells and mulching are the fix, and the next fruits recover even though the scarred ones will not.

Feed modestly. Maryland Extension recommends a starter fertilizer at transplanting and a side-dressing after the first fruits appear. NC State gives 2 to 3 tablespoons of a balanced fertilizer per plant, kept 4 to 6 inches from the stem, repeated every 4 to 6 weeks.

Too much nitrogen early gives a huge leafy plant with few flowers.

## When do you harvest tomatoes grown from seed?

Floradade ripens about 75 to 80 days after transplanting, so a plant set out in mid-May gives its first ripe fruit around the start of August. Pick when the fruit is fully colored and gives slightly to a squeeze, and pick often, because a plant with ripe fruit left on it slows down.

Before the first fall frost, pick every full-sized green tomato and ripen it indoors. Maryland Extension says mature green fruit stored at 50 to 70 degrees F keeps for one to three weeks while it colors. The plants come out at the frost, and the stakes go into storage until the sowing date comes around again in late winter.
