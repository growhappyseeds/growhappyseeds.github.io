Non-GMO, heirloom and organic are the three words most likely to make a seed packet look trustworthy, and they are not the same thing. Each answers a different question about the seed. Once you know the question, the labels stop competing.

Non-GMO is about how the variety was bred. Heirloom and open-pollinated are about whether saved seed grows true. Organic is about how the seed crop was farmed.

## What is the difference between non-GMO, heirloom and organic seeds?

Non-GMO means the variety was not genetically engineered, heirloom means an old open-pollinated variety, and organic means the seed came from certified organic plants. A single packet can be all three, two of them, or just one. They are layers, not rivals.

| Label | The question it answers | Who checks it | Does saved seed grow true? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Non-GMO | Was the variety genetically engineered? | Usually the seller; some packets add a third-party seal | Depends on whether it is open-pollinated or hybrid |
| Open-pollinated | Is the variety stable from saved seed? | The seller | Yes, if it was not cross-pollinated |
| Heirloom | Is it an open-pollinated variety with a long history? | No legal standard | Yes, if it was not cross-pollinated |
| Hybrid (F1) | Was it bred from a controlled cross of two parents? | The seller | No |
| Certified organic | Were the seed plants grown under organic rules? | A USDA-accredited certifier | Depends on the variety |

The rest of this guide takes each label in turn.

## What does non-GMO mean on a seed packet?

Non-GMO means the variety was bred without genetic engineering, which is true of almost every vegetable seed sold to home gardeners. North Carolina State Extension's Onslow County center explains in [GMO-free seeds](https://onslow.ces.ncsu.edu/gmo-free-seeds) that genetically engineered seed goes mostly to large farms. Its list is led by field crops such as corn, soybeans, cotton, alfalfa and canola.

There is now at least one exception. A bioengineered purple tomato went on sale to US home gardeners in 2024, as the [John Innes Centre reported](https://www.jic.ac.uk/news/us-gardeners-rush-to-snap-up-purple-tomatoes-pioneered-in-norfolk/). It is sold under its own name, not slipped into ordinary seed racks.

So a non-GMO label is accurate but rarely a point of difference. The Garden Professors, a group of university horticulturists, make the same point in their [guide to seed packet language](https://gardenprofessors.com/seedpackets/). It is still a fair thing to confirm, and it costs nothing to look for.

## What makes a seed heirloom?

A seed is heirloom when it is an open-pollinated variety with a long history, often described as 50 years or more of being passed down. Illinois Extension's guide to [choosing seed packets](https://extension.illinois.edu/news-releases/grow-confidence-when-choosing-seed-packets-your-garden) uses that 50-year line. The Garden Professors add that a family story can count as much as age.

There is no legal definition, so sellers draw the line in different places. What every definition shares is that an heirloom must be open-pollinated. That is the part that matters in the garden.

Heirlooms are prized for flavor, variety and history. They are not automatically easier to grow, and some lack the disease resistance bred into newer varieties.

## What is the difference between open-pollinated and hybrid seeds?

Open-pollinated seeds grow true to type from saved seed, while hybrid seeds do not. Illinois Extension explains that F1 hybrids come from breeders crossing two parent lines for traits like disease resistance. Seed saved from a hybrid grows into a variable mix of plants, often unlike the one you grew, so hybrid seed is bought fresh each year.

Hybrids are not GMOs. A hybrid is made by controlled pollination between plants of the same crop, the way breeders have worked for over a century. Many reliable garden varieties are hybrids.

Open-pollinated varieties include every heirloom plus many newer, stable varieties. If you want to save seed and grow the same plant next year, open-pollinated is the label to look for.

## What does certified organic mean for seeds?

Certified organic seed was harvested from plants grown on a farm certified under the USDA National Organic Program. The Garden Professors note that the label describes how the parent crop was produced. It says nothing about the variety itself, and organic does not mean pesticide-free.

Organic and heirloom are separate claims. Illinois Extension points out that many heirloom seeds are organically grown, but the heirloom label does not guarantee it. Look for the certified organic seal if that matters to you.

Organic seed is the right choice if you farm or sell certified organic produce, or want to support organic seed growers. In a home garden it generally grows the same as conventionally produced seed of the same variety.

## Which seed labels matter most for a home garden?

For a first home garden, the labels that matter most are a named variety, open-pollinated if you want to save seed, and a tested germination rate. Non-GMO is a given for nearly all garden seed. Heirloom and organic are worth paying for when they match what you value.

A germination rate tells you how many seeds in the packet are likely to sprout, and it is a number the seller measured. The guide on [what germination rate means](/blog/what-germination-rate-means/) explains how to read it, and [how to read a seed packet](/blog/how-to-read-a-seed-packet/) covers the rest of the label. Sowing dates still depend on your area, so confirm your frost dates with your county extension office.
