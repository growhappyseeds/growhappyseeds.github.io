This guide is for the balcony reader on an upper floor: a ledge in the sky with a railing, a view and air that never seems to stop moving. The constraint is wind, and behind it the things wind causes: dry pots, snapped stems, soil blown out of containers and fewer insects visiting. The workable answer is to garden low and heavy, shelter what you can and take over the bee's job where you must.

Everything here comes after the basics. Sun hours, pot sizes by crop and watering pots are covered in [the best vegetables for a balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/), and the rules and weight questions in the series posts linked below. This post is about what changes when the balcony is high.

## Why is a high balcony so much windier?

A high balcony is windier because the friction that slows wind near the ground fades with height. Meteorologist Jeff Haby's explanation of [why wind speed increases with height](https://www.theweatherprediction.com/habyhints3/749/) puts it plainly: trees, rocks and houses slow the air as it collides with them. That braking effect weakens the higher you go, and a tall building squeezes air around its corners, so gusts arrive from odd directions.

There is no single floor where this begins. A fourth-floor balcony behind other buildings can be calm and a tenth-floor balcony on an open corner can be brutal. Designers quoted in a Globe and Mail piece on [high-rise gardening](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/amp/life/home-and-garden/spring-cleaning/tips-for-high-rise-gardening/article4266735/) call wind more damaging than relentless sun and have seen it whip the soil out of a container.

The test is simple. Set a light empty plastic pot on the balcony on an ordinary breezy day. If it leaves, your plants will too, and the plan below applies.

## How do you keep pots from blowing over or drying out?

Keep pots upright with weight and a low profile, and keep them moist by watering every day. Harvest to Table's guide to [balcony and rooftop vegetable garden basics](https://harvesttotable.com/balcony-and-rooftop-vegetable-garden-basics/) notes that wind pulls moisture from both plants and containers.

It advises securing lightweight pots to railings and using high-walled containers. The Globe and Mail designers set the tallest pots at the back near the railing to shield smaller ones, and anchor large containers against the dividers between balconies.

Heavy is good for stability but bad for the slab. Read the series post on [balcony weight limits](/blog/balcony-weight-limits-for-containers/) before you buy a 20-gallon planter, and spread the load across the floor instead of one corner.

| Problem up high | What it does | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gusts | Tips light pots, snaps tall stems | Wide, low pots; stakes tied at several points; plants kept short or trellised |
| Constant breeze | Dries mix and leaves | Water daily, twice in summer; plastic or glazed pots; mulch the surface |
| Blown soil | Empties the top of the pot | Fill to an inch below the rim; mulch; a screen on the windward side |
| Fewer insects | Cucumbers and melons set no fruit | Hand pollinate in the morning |
| Cooler air | Slower spring start | Start seeds indoors; move out after frost |

A screen of woven reed or bamboo tied inside the railing softens wind without blocking light, Harvest to Table suggests. Anything you attach falls under your lease and house rules, so check the series post on [lease and building rules](/blog/balcony-garden-lease-and-building-rules/) first. Never block an exit, and never use a fire escape for plants.

## Does a high balcony have fewer bees, and does it matter?

A high balcony sees fewer bees, and it matters for cucumbers and melons but not for tomatoes, peppers or eggplant. Colorado State University Extension's page on [hand pollination](https://pueblo.extension.colostate.edu/hand-pollination/) explains that tomato, pepper and eggplant flowers hold both parts and need only a little air movement to self-pollinate. Wind knocks the pollen loose, so a breezy balcony helps them.

Cucumbers and melons carry separate male and female flowers. Washington State University Extension's note on [pollination failure in cucumbers and squash](https://hortsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/?p=3725) says both kinds of flower are needed and the earliest females often go unpollinated. Unpollinated fruit stays small, yellows and drops, and up high, where few bees cruise past, that is common.

The fix takes a minute each morning. Pick a male flower, the one on a thin plain stem, strip its petals and dab its center onto each open female, which has a miniature fruit behind the bloom. One male can serve several females, WSU notes, and the series post on [cucumbers on a balcony trellis](/blog/cucumbers-on-a-balcony-railing-trellis/) has the full routine.

Heat can fake a pollination problem. Colorado State says tomato pollen turns sterile when days pass roughly 85 to 90 degrees F and nights stay above 75, so flowers drop unfertilized. That is a hot-balcony issue, covered in the series post on [heat-island balconies](/blog/hot-balcony-heat-island-vegetables/), not a bee shortage.

## Is it colder on a high balcony?

A high balcony is usually cooler and windier than the street in spring and fall, so the season starts a little later. The Globe and Mail designers put it in one line: the higher up you are, the colder and windier it will be. Wind also strips heat from leaves and pots, so a 50-degree evening up high is harsher on a seedling than the same evening in a sheltered yard.

Start warm-season seeds indoors and move them out only after your last frost date, confirmed with your county extension office. The [state planting calendars](/planting/) give the window to count from, and the series post on [starting indoors and finishing on the balcony](/blog/start-indoors-move-to-balcony/) covers hardening off. Give transplants a week of calm-day outings before they face the gusts full time.

Summer flips the picture. Concrete and glass store heat, and Toronto Master Gardeners warn a [balcony gardener](https://www.torontomastergardeners.ca/?p=16879) that plants can face temperatures higher than the thermometer says. Light-colored pots and daily watering help on those days.

## What should you plant from seed on a high balcony?

Plant low, sturdy crops first and stake or trellis anything tall. Harvest to Table lists low-growing vegetables such as greens, carrots and dwarf beans as the ones that tolerate wind. In practice that means spinach, kale harvested young, beets, turnips and carrots in a deep pot, all under knee height.

| Crop | Wind tolerance | Pollination up high | Pot and support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spinach, kale, beet, turnip | High, stays low | Not needed for leaves and roots | Window box, or a pot 10 inches deep for beets, no support |
| Carrot | High | Not needed | 12-inch-deep pot, no support |
| Tomato | Medium, must be staked | Self-pollinates in moving air | 5 gallon or larger, stake tied at several heights |
| Pepper, eggplant | Medium | Self-pollinates; tap flowers on still days | 3 to 5 gallon for pepper, 5 gallon for eggplant, one stake |
| Cucumber | Medium, tie often | Hand pollinate | 5 gallon with a trellis the building allows |
| Melon | Low, heavy and sprawling | Hand pollinate | 10 gallon or more; often not practical up high |

Tomatoes work on a high balcony when the pot is heavy and the stake is tied at several heights, as the series post on [tomatoes for a small balcony](/blog/tomatoes-on-a-small-balcony/) shows. Peppers and eggplant are shorter and sturdier; on still days, tap their flowers as the Colorado State page suggests, eggplant in the morning and peppers around midday. Melons are the honest exception: a 10-gallon pot of wet mix and a sprawling vine are a lot to ask of a windy ledge.
