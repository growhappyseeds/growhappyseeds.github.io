This guide is for the apartment gardener with a balcony, a small terrace or a roof deck the building allows. It assumes a tray of seedlings that has only ever known a windowsill. Those seedlings cannot go straight outside: they need one to two weeks of short, lengthening visits first, a step called hardening off.

A balcony changes how that step works: there is no tree to sit under, the wind is often stronger than at street level, and the floor is concrete. If you have no outdoor space at all, start with [how to grow vegetables from seed in an apartment with no balcony](/blog/apartment-vegetable-garden-from-seed/).

## What is hardening off, and can balcony seedlings skip it?

Hardening off is the gradual exposure of indoor seedlings to outdoor sun, wind and temperature, and balcony seedlings cannot skip it. The University of Maryland Extension's page on [hardening off vegetable seedlings](https://extension.umd.edu/resource/hardening-vegetable-seedlings-home-garden) describes a change from soft, succulent growth to firmer, harder growth.

Iowa State University Extension's guide to [hardening and planting bedding plants](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/how-to/selecting-hardening-and-planting-bedding-plants) is plain about the risk: intense sun and strong winds may damage or kill tender seedlings. A balcony usually has more of both than a sheltered backyard. The basic method is in our post on [when to start seeds indoors](/blog/when-to-start-seeds-indoors/); this guide adapts it to a balcony.

## When should apartment seedlings start going outside?

Start hardening one to two weeks before each crop's planting-out date: about the last spring frost date for tomatoes, and up to a week later for peppers and eggplant. Maryland Extension gives that one-to-two-week lead time. Find your frost date on the [state planting calendars](/planting/) and confirm it with your county extension office.

Not every crop makes the trip. The Happy Seeds Veggie Garden Seed Kit shows the split. Three of its ten varieties are started indoors, melon can be, and the rest are sown straight into their outdoor containers.

| Crop | How it reaches the balcony | Planting-out time | Hardening needed? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tomato (Floradade) | Started indoors 6 to 8 weeks before last frost | 1 to 2 weeks after last frost | Yes |
| Pepper (Anaheim Chili), eggplant (Black Beauty) | Started indoors 8 weeks before last frost | 2 weeks after last frost | Yes, on warm days only |
| Cucumber (Marketmore 76) | Sown straight into its balcony pot | 1 to 2 weeks after last frost, in warm mix | No |
| Melon (Honeydew Orange) | Sown in its pot, or started indoors 3 weeks before transplanting | 2 weeks after last frost, in warm mix | Only if started indoors |
| Kale, spinach, carrot, beet, turnip | Sown straight into a balcony planter | 2 to 6 weeks before last frost, by crop | No |

The five cool-season packets grow in a sunny window or a balcony planter. The five warm-season packets need a big container and real sun, which is why they end up outside. The box holds the seeds and the grow guide and nothing else, so trays, pots and potting mix are yours to add.

Cold decides the rest, and a frost date is only an average, so watch the forecast. Maryland Extension says not to put tender seedlings outdoors when temperatures are below 45 degrees F. Cornell's [tomato growing guide](https://gardening.cals.cornell.edu/garden-guidance/foodgarden/vegetable-growing-guides/tomato-growing-guide/) wants nights consistently above 45 before tomatoes are planted.

Eggplant needs more warmth. Illinois Extension's [eggplant page](https://extension.illinois.edu/gardening/eggplant) says to plant when nights reliably stay above 50 degrees F, and calls eggplant more easily injured by cold than tomato. Peppers are grown the same way, so hold peppers, eggplant and melon to 50.

## Where is the shaded, sheltered spot on a balcony?

The shaded, sheltered spot on most balconies is the floor against the building wall, at the hours when the wall or the balcony above shades it. Extension advice usually says to start seedlings under a tree or a covered porch. A balcony has neither, so watch where the shade actually falls in the afternoon before the first trip out.

Railings with open bars stop almost no wind, and a top-floor balcony or roof deck has no overhang for shade. Stand a cardboard box on its side with the opening facing the wall, weigh it down, and set the tray inside as a windbreak and sunshade in one. Keep trays off the railing and off any ledge, where one gust can send them over the edge.

Concrete is the other balcony problem. Virginia Cooperative Extension's guide to [vegetable gardening in containers](https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/content/dam/pubs_ext_vt_edu/426/426-336/SPES-796.pdf) warns about heat reflected from pavement and suggests raising containers so air can move beneath them. Set the tray on a board or an upturned crate, and check it twice on hot or windy days, because small cells dry out fast.

Never use the fire escape as the halfway house. It is an emergency exit, and most city fire codes require it to stay clear.

## A 10-day balcony hardening schedule

Most seedlings are ready in about ten days: two in shade, two with gentle sun, three of longer days in the open, then full days and nights. Iowa State puts the job at 7 to 10 days. The University of Minnesota Extension's guide to [starting seeds indoors](https://extension.umn.edu/gardening-minnesota/starting-seeds-indoors) allows two weeks, which is the better choice in a cold or windy spring.

| Days | Where on the balcony | How long | Bring the tray in |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 2 | Floor against the wall, in shade, out of the wind | A few hours in the warm part of the afternoon | Before the evening cools |
| 3 to 4 | Same spot, moved to catch early-morning or late-day sun | About half the day | Before dusk |
| 5 to 7 | The open part of the balcony | Most of the day, with more direct sun each day | At night |
| 8 to 10 | Where the final containers will stand | All day, then overnight if nights stay above 50 degrees F | Only for cold, storms or strong wind |

Hardly anyone is home on a weekday afternoon to carry trays, so stretch the schedule to two weeks and anchor it on weekends. Do days 1 to 2 on a Saturday and Sunday. On workdays, leave the tray in a sheltered spot that stays shaded all day, and only when the forecast is mild and calm.

Save the first days in the open for the second weekend, when you are home to watch for wilting. Maryland Extension describes the same approach: a shaded, protected spot on warm days, in at night, and more sunlight each day.

Skip a day rather than push through bad weather. Maryland Extension says to keep tender seedlings in on windy days, and Iowa State says to move plants indoors if strong winds, a severe storm or frost threatens. This is where an apartment wins: the door is three steps away, and one rimmed baking sheet carries the whole tray.

Water a little less often during these days, but never let the plants wilt. Cornell notes that tomatoes are hardened by reducing water and fertilizer, not by exposing them to cold. Maryland Extension says melons and cucumbers raised indoors may stay out overnight only if it will not fall below 50 degrees F.

## How do you move seedlings into their final balcony containers?

Transplant in the evening or on a cloudy day, into containers that are already standing where they will stay. Iowa State gives that timing because it lets plants recover before they face direct sun. A large pot of wet mix is too heavy to shift later, so fill it in place.

Before the big pots go out, check your lease, the building rules and the balcony's weight limit, because several large wet containers add up. Put a saucer under each one. Virginia Cooperative Extension notes that water draining from an upstairs balcony can become a problem for the neighbors below.

Pot sizes for each crop are in [the best vegetables to grow on a balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/). Set tomatoes deeper than they grew in the starter pot. Cornell says to bury the stem until the soil is just below the lowest leaves, and roots will form along the buried part.

Peppers and eggplant go in at the same depth they were growing. Add the stake or cage on planting day; Virginia Cooperative Extension advises giving supports while plants are very small to avoid root damage later. Water each plant in well.

## What do scorched, wilted or purple leaves mean?

Scorched leaves mean too much sun too soon, wilting means sun shock or thirst, and deep purple means cold; the fix for each is to go back a stage. White or tan papery patches on the leaves are sunscald. Those leaves will not recover, but new growth comes in normal if the tray goes back to shade for a couple of days.

Minnesota Extension says hardened-off plants may wilt when first exposed to full sun and generally recover within a day or so. Wilting in dry mix is simply thirst, so water and check the tray more often.

Purplish veins on tomato leaves are common and usually harmless. Clemson Cooperative Extension's [tomato factsheet](https://hgic.clemson.edu/factsheet/tomato/) notes that tomato foliage develops them as plants harden off. Leaves that turn deep purple all over point to cold, so keep the tray in on cool nights.

Snapped or flattened stems mean the wind got in, so rebuild the windbreak before the next trip outside.
