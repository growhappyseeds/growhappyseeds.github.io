Vegetable seed kits range from a handful of unlabeled packets in a cardboard sleeve to a curated box with a guide and tested seed. The box art rarely tells you which one you are holding. These seven checks do, and most of them take less than a minute on the product page or the back of the packet.

If you are still deciding between a kit and loose packets, [Seed Kit vs Seed Packets](/blog/seed-kit-vs-seed-packets/) covers that question first. This guide assumes you want a kit and need to tell a good one from a weak one.

## The seven checks at a glance

A good kit names its varieties, prints a germination rate, holds enough seed per packet and includes a real guide. It also uses non-GMO open-pollinated seed, says which crops grow in containers, and arrives ready to give. The table shows what to look for on each point and what should make you put the box down.

| Check | Look for | Walk away if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Variety count and mix | 8 to 12 crops, split between cool-season and warm-season | Only "mixed greens" or only summer crops |
| 2. Named varieties | A variety name on every packet, such as Marketmore 76 cucumber | Packets that say only "Cucumber" |
| 3. Seeds per packet | A stated total, around 50 to 150 per crop for a small garden | No seed count anywhere on the box or listing |
| 4. Germination rate printed | A percentage and a packed-for year on each packet | No rate, no date, or a "Below Standard" mark |
| 5. Guide quality | A booklet that covers timing by frost date, depth, spacing and care | A single card with "plant in spring" |
| 6. Non-GMO and open-pollinated | Both terms stated on the packaging | Vague claims like "natural" with nothing specific |
| 7. Container fit and gift-readiness | Pot sizes per crop; a box that needs no wrapping | Crops that need a large plot; packets loose in a bag |

The rest of the guide explains why each check matters and how to run it.

## 1. Variety count: enough crops to fill a season

Eight to twelve varieties is a practical range because a first garden needs crops for spring, summer and fall, not twelve kinds of tomato. Fewer than six leaves the garden empty for part of the year. More than fifteen is hard to plant in a raised bed or a few containers.

The mix matters more than the number. Cool-season crops such as spinach, kale, beets, turnips and carrots go in before the last frost and again in late summer. Warm-season crops such as tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant and melon wait until the soil is warm.

A kit that balances those two groups gives you early harvests from the greens while the fruiting crops take their time. The Happy Seeds Veggie Garden Seed Kit uses five cool-season and five warm-season varieties for exactly this reason. [What Are the Best Vegetable Seeds for Beginners?](/blog/best-vegetable-seeds-for-beginners/) explains the logic behind that split.

## 2. Named varieties: the crop name alone is not enough

Every packet should carry a variety name, because "tomato" could be a 55-day cherry or a 90-day beefsteak with very different needs. A named variety lets you look up days to maturity, disease resistance and whether it suits containers.

Named varieties also signal that the seed came from a real seed lot rather than a bulk bin. Catalog names like Danvers 126 carrot, Bloomsdale spinach or Black Beauty eggplant have decades of growing records behind them. If the kit's product page lists only crop names, ask the seller which varieties are inside before you buy.

## 3. Seeds per packet: more is not better

Fifty to 150 seeds of most crops is enough for a small garden. A kit that states its seed count is easier to trust than one that does not. A 4-by-8-foot bed might use a dozen beets, 10 kale plants and 2 or 3 tomato plants in a season, plus a short row of carrots sown thickly and thinned.

Full retail packets often hold hundreds of seeds of one crop, which is more than a beginner can plant before the seed ages. Kits usually size packets for a small garden instead. A box with 1,000 or more seeds across ten packets averages about 100 per crop, which covers a first garden plus a second sowing.

Check that the count is for the whole kit, not a single packet. "1,000 seeds" spread over ten packets and "1,000 seeds" in one packet of lettuce are very different kits.

## 4. A printed germination rate: the check most kits fail

A germination rate printed on the packet tells you how many seeds sprouted in a recent lab test. It is the single best sign that a kit sells tested seed.

Under [7 CFR 201.29](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/7/201.29), seed sold across state lines in packets of one pound or less need not show a germination percentage if it meets the federal minimum. A kit that prints the rate is volunteering information the law lets it skip.

The federal minimums themselves, set in [7 CFR 201.31](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/7/201.31), vary by crop. Any packet below the minimum must be marked "Below Standard" and show its actual rate.

| Crop | Federal minimum germination |
| --- | --- |
| Cucumber, turnip, lettuce, pea | 80% |
| Tomato, kale, melon (not watermelon), radish, squash | 75% |
| Beet | 65% |
| Spinach, eggplant | 60% |
| Watermelon | 70% |
| Carrot, pepper | 55% |

Alongside the rate, look for a packed-for year. A packet marked "packed for 2026" was packaged for this season rather than carried over from an earlier one.

Leftover seed still keeps for years when stored cool and dry; [Nebraska Extension](https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2090/na/html/view) lists typical storage life by crop. [What Does Germination Rate Mean on a Seed Packet?](/blog/what-germination-rate-means/) explains how to use the number when you sow.

## 5. Guide quality: the difference between a kit and a bag of packets

A real grow guide is what makes a kit worth more than its packets. The back of a packet has room for three lines, and a first season needs more than that. A good guide covers sowing time relative to your last frost date, planting depth, spacing, thinning, watering and what a healthy seedling looks like.

Judge the guide by page count and by whether it is specific to the varieties in the box. A 24-page booklet written for ten named crops is useful. A folded card that says "plant after danger of frost" is not.

Whatever the guide says, confirm your last and first frost dates with your county extension office before you plant. The [state planting pages](/planting/) on this site give a starting window for every state, including [New York](/planting/new-york.html) and [New Jersey](/planting/new-jersey.html). Local frost dates vary by several weeks within a state.

## 6. Non-GMO and open-pollinated: what the labels mean

Non-GMO and open-pollinated are the two labels that matter for a home garden kit, and they are not the same as organic or heirloom. Non-GMO means the variety was not genetically engineered. Open-pollinated means the plants are pollinated naturally and their saved seed grows true to type.

Hybrid seed is a deliberate cross between two parent varieties. According to [NC State Extension](https://caldwell.ces.ncsu.edu/news/a-guide-for-seed-buying/), hybrids will not produce consistent offspring from saved seed, while open-pollinated varieties can be saved if they are kept from crossing. Hybrids are not GMOs, but saved hybrid seed does not grow true, so you buy fresh seed each year.

Organic is a certification of how the seed crop was grown. It is a fine thing to look for, but it says nothing about whether the variety is named, tested or suited to a beginner. For a first garden, named open-pollinated non-GMO seed with a printed germination rate is the stronger set of claims.

## 7. Container fit and gift-readiness

A kit for a first garden should say which crops grow in containers and what pot size each needs, because many first gardens are a balcony or a patio. Greens and root crops are happy in 10 to 12 inches of soil. Peppers want a 3 to 5 gallon pot, tomatoes, cucumbers and eggplant want 5 gallons, and melons need 10 gallons or more.

If the kit's listing gives those sizes, the brand has thought about how beginners actually grow. If it does not, assume some crops will need ground space. [The Best Vegetables to Grow on a Balcony](/blog/best-vegetables-to-grow-on-a-balcony/) lists which crops cope with small pots.

Gift-readiness is the last check, because seed kits are often bought for someone else. A box with a drawer or lid, a guide inside and packets that stand up on their own can be handed over as is. Loose packets in a plastic sleeve need a container and a card before they feel like a present.
