Six hours of direct sun a day is the figure that separates a vegetable garden that produces from one that only grows leaves. Iowa State Extension's answer on [vegetables for partial shade](https://yardandgarden.extension.iastate.edu/faq/which-vegetables-will-grow-partial-shade) puts it plainly: most vegetables need at least 6 hours of direct sun each day to make a good crop.

That one number hides a useful range. Fruiting crops want more, leafy crops accept less, and the hours can be split across the day. Below are the hours by crop, what happens with less, and how to measure the sun a bed, balcony or window gets.

## How many hours of sun do vegetables need?

Vegetables need at least 6 hours of direct sun for fruit, 4 or so for roots, and 3 to 4 for leaves. The University of Saskatchewan's guide to [assessing sunlight](https://gardening.usask.ca/articles-and-lists/articles-how-to/assess-sunlight.php) defines the terms used on seed packets and plant tags, and they are worth memorizing.

| Term on the packet | Direct sun per day | What it grows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full sun | 6 hours or more | Everything, and fruiting crops want 8 or more |
| Partial sun | 4 to 6 hours | Greens, roots, and small harvests of fruiting crops |
| Partial shade | 2 to 4 hours | Leafy greens at the top of that range, slowly |
| Shade | Under 2 hours | No vegetables |

The hours are cumulative. Saskatchewan's example is 4 hours of morning sun, a few hours of dappled shade, then 3 more hours in the afternoon, which counts as 7 hours of full sun. A bed that is bright but never in direct sun earns no hours, though reflected light helps at the margin.

## Which vegetables need full sun?

Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, melons and squash need full sun, and the UC Master Gardeners of Placer County say they do best with [8 to 10 hours](https://ucanr.edu/site/uc-master-gardeners-placer-county/article/growing-vegetables-less-6-hours-sun). The same page calls it unwise to attempt them with less than 6 hours.

Fruit is expensive for a plant to make, and Saskatchewan's guide says any plant that produces one needs at least 6 hours and yields more with much more than that. A tomato in partial shade sets less fruit than one in full sun. The plant will look fine in 5 hours; it will not pay you back.

## Which vegetables grow in partial shade?

Leafy greens grow in partial shade, and root crops manage with a little more light. Iowa State lists leaf lettuce, spinach, collards, Swiss chard and kale as crops that succeed with 3 to 4 hours of sun. It adds radishes, kohlrabi, beets, cauliflower and broccoli as other options.

The Placer County Master Gardeners say about 4 hours is worth trying for radishes, beets, carrots, broccoli, Brussels sprouts and cauliflower, and as little as 3 hours for leafy vegetables. They explain why greens cope: you can pick a leaf at any stage, so a plant that never reaches full size still feeds you. The cost is thinner leaves and a less robust plant.

| Crop in the kit | Sun it wants | What less sun does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kale (Red Russian) | 6 hours best, 3 to 4 workable | Smaller, slower leaves |
| Spinach (Bloomsdale) | 6 hours best, 3 to 4 workable | Smaller leaves, but slower to bolt in summer |
| Beet (Chioggia) | 6 hours best, about 4 workable | Good greens, small or late roots |
| Turnip (Purple Top White Globe) | 6 hours best, about 4 workable | Good greens, small or late roots |
| Carrot (Danvers 126) | 6 hours best, about 4 workable | Thin, slow roots |

Shade has one upside. Placer County notes that crops quick to bolt in hot weather, such as lettuce and spinach, last quite a bit longer with some shade, and the beds need less watering. The site also warns that shade under a tree comes with tree roots that take the water and nutrients first.

## What happens when vegetables get less sun than they need?

With less sun than it needs a vegetable grows smaller, matures later and yields less, and a fruiting crop may set almost nothing. Saskatchewan's guide says vegetables with under 6 hours of full sun are smaller and give a smaller crop; the Placer County page says no vegetable grows in full shade.

The symptoms are easy to read. Stems stretch toward the brightest side, leaves come out thin and pale, and flowering crops drop blossoms or hold green fruit for weeks. ## How do you measure the sun your garden gets?

Measure sun by watching one spot for a full day and adding up the hours it sits in direct light. Saskatchewan's method is to photograph the garden every hour from dawn to dusk, or keep a chart, marking each hour as full sun, partial shade, dappled sun or full shade.

1. Pick a clear day in late spring, after your last frost; Saskatchewan says that is the best time of year to assess a garden.
2. Every hour, note whether the spot is in direct sun, bright but shaded, dappled under leaves, or fully shaded.
3. Add the direct-sun hours. Dappled and bright shade are a bonus, not part of the total.
4. Repeat in midsummer. Placer County reminds gardeners that the sun hits the garden at different angles as the seasons progress.
5. Check again every few years, because trees and new buildings change the light.

Two refinements help small spaces. Reflected light off a pale building or fence lightens shade, Saskatchewan notes, so a bed against a white wall reads brighter than the hour count suggests. And the Placer County page suggests growing up, with a trellis or hanging containers on a high post, to catch sun that never reaches the ground.

## How do you plan a garden around the sun you have?

Put the fruiting crops in the sunniest spot and the greens where the sun runs out, and time the greens for the cool half of the year. A bed with 6 hours gets the tomato, pepper and cucumber; a bed with 4 hours gets kale, spinach, beets and turnips.

If the whole garden gets 4 hours, skip fruiting crops the first year and grow greens and roots, which is also the honest answer for a shaded balcony. The cool-season crops are direct sown 2 to 6 weeks before the last spring frost and again in late summer. Confirm your frost dates with your county extension office or the [state planting calendars](/planting/) before you sow.
