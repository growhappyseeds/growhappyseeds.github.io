Balcony growing

The best vegetables to grow on a balcony or a windowsill.

By Happy Seeds · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

For apartment gardeners with a railing, a sill and a few pots: which vegetables fit, how big each pot needs to be, and how to keep them watered.

The short answerThe best vegetables to grow on a balcony are the ones that stay small and don't mind a pot: leafy greens like kale and spinach, root crops like carrots, beets and turnips, and, if you get six hours of sun, a tomato, a pepper or a cucumber in a bigger container. Greens and roots are happy in a pot 8 to 12 inches deep on a windowsill or in a railing planter. Fruiting crops need a 5-gallon pot or larger and full sun. Water pots more often than you would a garden bed, because they dry out fast.

How much sun does a balcony vegetable garden need?

Most vegetables want six hours of direct sun a day, and leafy greens get by on three to four. That one number decides what you can grow, so measure it before you buy a pot. On a clear day, step out at 9, noon and 3 and note where the light actually lands. The balcony above you and the building next door cast shade you won't notice until you look for it.

A south- or west-facing balcony usually gives you the six hours a tomato, pepper or cucumber needs. East-facing gets soft morning sun, plenty for greens and roots. North-facing is the hard case, and there's a section on it below.

Balconies have one thing a garden doesn't: walls. A pale wall bounces light onto the leaves. A dark wall soaks up heat and gives it back at night, which tomatoes love and spinach hates.

How do wind and heat change things on a balcony?

Wind dries a pot faster than sun does, so plan around it second. An eighth-floor balcony gets a steady breeze a backyard never sees, and it pulls water out of leaves, topples tall plants and snaps tomato stems heavy with fruit.

  • Put your heaviest, widest pots on the windy side to shelter the small ones.
  • Keep pots low and close together. A cluster shades its own soil and holds humidity.
  • Stake anything that will pass knee height on planting day, not the day it falls over. Tie the stake to the railing if you can.
  • Choose pots with some weight. A plastic pot of dry mix weighs almost nothing and will travel.

Heat is the other side of the coin. Concrete holds warmth, and a balcony can run several degrees hotter than the street. A dark pot in July can cook the roots along its sunny side. If your balcony bakes, use light-colored pots, or slip a dark pot inside a bigger pale one.

What are the easiest vegetables to grow on a balcony or windowsill?

The easiest balcony vegetables are kale, spinach, carrots, beets and turnips. They don't need pollinating or a trellis, they're fine with four hours of sun, and they fit in a railing planter or on a windowsill.

  • Kale 'Red Russian' has flat, blue-green leaves with purple veins and a more tender bite than curly kale. Pick the outer leaves and it keeps producing for months. It shrugs off frost, so it's often the last plant standing in fall.
  • Spinach 'Bloomsdale' is the fast one. Sow a pinch every two weeks for a steady supply of crinkled leaves. It bolts in summer heat, so grow it in spring and again in fall.
  • Carrot 'Danvers 126' is a medium-length carrot that copes with a pot better than the long kinds. Carrots are slow to germinate, two to three weeks sometimes, and the surface has to stay damp the whole time. That's normal. Once they're up, thin to two inches apart.
  • Beet 'Chioggia' shows candy-striped rings when you slice it. Each beet seed is a cluster, so thin the seedlings and eat the thinnings as greens.
  • Turnip 'Purple Top' is a two-for-one: peppery greens in a few weeks, then a purple-and-white root. In a pot, pull them small and sweet.

If you have six hours of sun and room for bigger pots, add the summer three. Tomato 'Floradade' is a determinate type: it grows to a set size and ripens over a few weeks instead of sprawling all season, which is what you want in a container. Pepper 'Anaheim Chili' stays compact and takes heat well. Cucumber 'Marketmore 76' is a vine, so give it a trellis tied to the railing and it climbs up instead of out.

How often should you water vegetables in pots?

Water a pot when the top inch of mix is dry, which in summer means every day for a small pot and every other day for a 5-gallon one. Don't water on a schedule; water on a finger test. Push a finger in to the second knuckle. Dry means water now.

When you do water, keep going until it runs out the bottom, then stop. A quick splash wets the top inch and leaves the roots dry. Morning is best, so the plant has water on board before the heat. Keep a saucer under each pot, but tip it out an hour later so the roots aren't sitting in a puddle.

A few things make the rhythm easier:

  1. Mulch the surface with an inch of straw or bark. It cuts evaporation sharply.
  2. Group pots together. Shaded soil stays damp longer.
  3. Go bigger. The most common reason a balcony tomato wilts is a pot that's too small.
  4. Feed. Potting mix runs out of nutrients in six to eight weeks; a half-strength liquid feed every two weeks keeps fruiting crops going. In a heat wave, a potted tomato can drink a gallon a day.

What can a north-facing balcony still grow?

A north-facing balcony can still grow spinach, kale and turnip and beet greens, as long as it gets open sky and three or more hours of bright light. Skip tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers and anything else that has to flower and set fruit. Grow for leaves, not roots, and pick them young.

Spinach 'Bloomsdale' and Kale 'Red Russian' are the two to start with. Sow them a little thicker than the packet says and cut the whole planter at three or four inches, like a salad mix. Turnip 'Purple Top' sown the same way gives mild, mustardy greens, and Beet 'Chioggia' gives red-stemmed baby leaves that look expensive in a salad.

Get every bit of light you can. Hang planters on the outside of the railing, where they clear the overhang, and prop white foam board behind the pots to bounce light back. Expect everything to take about half again as long as in sun. On an indoor windowsill, a south-facing window is roughly a partly shaded balcony; a clip-on LED grow light for twelve hours a day closes the gap for a tray of greens.

A small-space planting plan for one balcony

Here is a plan for a three-by-eight-foot balcony with six hours of sun: six containers, one windowsill, eight of the ten packets in the kit.

  1. Railing planter, 30 inches long and 8 inches deep. Spinach 'Bloomsdale' in one half, Kale 'Red Russian' in the other.
  2. Two 10-inch pots. Carrot 'Danvers 126' in one, Beet 'Chioggia' in the other, somewhere you'll remember to keep the surface damp.
  3. One 5-gallon pot in the sunniest corner. Tomato 'Floradade', one plant, staked on planting day.
  4. One 3-gallon pot beside it. Pepper 'Anaheim Chili', which likes the heat.
  5. One 5-gallon pot against the wall, with a trellis. Cucumber 'Marketmore 76', trained upward.
  6. Windowsill trough inside. Turnip 'Purple Top' sown thick for greens.

On timing: sow the greens and roots directly in their pots once nights stay above about 40°F, usually a few weeks before your last frost. Start the tomato, pepper and cucumber indoors six to eight weeks ahead of that frost and move them out once nights hold above 50°F; when to start seeds indoors walks through it. Common questions

Do balcony tomatoes need to be hand-pollinated?

Usually not. Tomatoes are self-pollinating and the breeze on a balcony shakes the flowers enough to set fruit. If flowers keep dropping without setting, tap the stem gently at midday every day or two to help the pollen along.

Can I use soil from the garden in balcony pots?

No. Garden soil packs down hard in a container, drains poorly and can carry pests indoors. Use a bagged potting mix, which stays loose and holds water evenly, and refresh it each season.

How deep does a pot need to be to grow carrots?

Ten to twelve inches for a medium carrot like 'Danvers 126'. Shallower than that and the roots fork or stop short. A loose, stone-free mix matters as much as depth.

Can vegetables grow on a windowsill without a grow light?

Leafy greens can. A south-facing window gives spinach, kale and baby turnip greens enough light to grow slowly through winter. Tomatoes and peppers need far more light than a window provides, so plan on a grow light or a sunny balcony for those.

Will my balcony hold the weight of vegetable pots?

Almost always, if you keep pots along the walls and railing rather than clustered in the middle. A 5-gallon pot of wet mix weighs about 40 pounds, roughly a person standing there. Check with your building before adding more than a dozen large pots.
