First garden

How to start a vegetable garden for beginners.

By Happy Seeds · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

For anyone who has never grown food before: where to put the garden, what to sow first, how deep, how often to water, and what to expect by when.

The short answerStart a vegetable garden by picking the spot that gets the most sun, at least six hours a day, and growing in a few large containers or one small raised bed. Sow six forgiving crops first: a tomato, a cucumber, kale, spinach, carrots and beets. Plant each seed about two to three times as deep as it is wide, keep the soil evenly moist, and expect leafy greens in roughly four to six weeks and tomatoes two to three months after planting out.

Where should a first vegetable garden go?

Put it in the sunniest spot you have. Most vegetables want six to eight hours of direct sun a day, and fruiting crops like tomatoes and cucumbers do best at the top of that range. Leafy greens and root crops will manage on four to six.

Do not guess. On a clear day, check the spot at 9 a.m., noon, 3 p.m. and 6 p.m. and note whether it is in sun or shade. Add it up. A south-facing wall or a balcony that faces south or west usually wins. A spot under a tree or on the north side of a fence usually loses.

Two other things matter. Keep the garden close to a tap, because a garden you have to carry water to gets watered less. And keep it somewhere you walk past daily, because a garden you have to go looking for gets forgotten by July.

If the sunniest spot you own is a balcony or a windowsill, that is still a garden. Leafy and root crops suit a sunny windowsill or balcony planter well, and the balcony growing guide covers which ones and what size pot each needs.

Should you grow in containers or a raised bed?

Start with containers if you rent, have a patio, or are not sure you will keep going; start with one small raised bed if you have ground and plan to garden again next year. The difference is cost, effort and space.

Whatever you choose, size matters. A pot for a tomato should hold at least 20 litres (5 gallons); greens are happy in something 20 cm (8 in) deep; carrots and beets want 25 to 30 cm (10 to 12 in) so the roots have room. Fill containers with potting mix, not garden soil, which packs down in a pot. Fill a bed with a blend of topsoil and compost.

How much space do you need in total? Less than you think. One 1.2 m by 1.2 m bed, or five large pots, will grow all six of the beginner crops below. Start small on purpose. The most common reason a first garden fails is not bad soil or bad luck. It is being too big to keep up with.

What should you grow first?

Grow a mix of two fruiting crops, two leafy crops and two root crops, so something is ready early while the slow ones catch up. These six are forgiving, well known, and all of them are in the Veggie Garden Kit:

  • Tomato 'Floradade'. A dependable slicing tomato bred for heat, so it keeps setting fruit when other varieties sulk. Needs the sunniest spot, a stake or cage, and a big pot or a bed.
  • Cucumber 'Marketmore 76'. A classic dark-green slicer. On a trellis or string it takes up almost no floor space.
  • Kale 'Red Russian'. Flat, purple-veined leaves that stay tender. Pick the outer leaves and it keeps producing for months, and it shrugs off light frost.
  • Spinach 'Bloomsdale'. Crinkled, dark leaves. Fast in cool weather, fussy in heat, so sow it early in spring or again in late summer.
  • Carrot 'Danvers 126'. A stout, blunt-ended carrot that copes with heavier soil better than the long types. Slow to sprout. That is normal.
  • Beet 'Chioggia'. The candy-striped one. Roots and young leaves are both edible, so one row gives two harvests.

Hold off on peppers, eggplant and melons in year one unless you have a long, hot summer; they are slow and heat-hungry. If you do have the heat, Pepper 'Anaheim Chili' and Eggplant 'Black Beauty' are in the same box and the guide covers both.

How do you sow seeds and water them?

Sow each seed about two to three times as deep as it is wide, then keep the soil evenly moist until it sprouts. That one rule handles almost everything: tiny carrot seed is barely covered, and a fat cucumber seed goes in about 2 cm (an inch) down.

  1. Read the packet. It tells you the depth, spacing, and whether to sow outside or start indoors. Good packets also print the germination rate, which tells you how many seeds to expect to sprout. Here is how to read a seed packet.
  2. Check the soil temperature. Spinach and kale sprout in cool soil around 10 C (50 F). Carrots and beets like 10 to 25 C (50 to 75 F). Tomatoes and cucumbers want 20 C (68 F) and up, which is why they are usually started indoors or sown after the last frost. For a head start, see when to start seeds indoors.
  3. Sow a little thick, then thin. Drop two or three seeds where you want one plant. Once they have a second set of leaves, snip the extras at soil level with scissors. Pulling them disturbs the one you keep.
  4. Water gently and often at first. Use a fine spray or a can with a rose. Seeds that dry out after they start to swell are done for. Once seedlings are a few inches tall, switch to deeper, less frequent watering so the roots chase it down.
  5. Use the finger test. Push a finger into the soil to the second knuckle. Dry at the tip? Water. Damp? Wait. Pots in summer usually need this daily; a bed every two or three days. Water the soil, not the leaves, and in the morning if you can.

What does a first season look like?

A first season runs from a few weeks before your last spring frost to a few weeks after your first fall frost. Your last-frost date is the hinge. Look yours up by zip code, then count from it. Shift everything earlier for a warm region and later for a cold one. For typical frost timing where you live, see when to plant vegetables in your state, then confirm the date with your county extension office.

What mistakes do beginners make?

The big ones are too much shade, too much garden, too many seeds in one spot, and uneven watering. Every gardener has made all four.

  • Choosing the pretty spot over the sunny spot. A tomato in four hours of sun gives you a leafy green plant and three fruit. Measure first.
  • Going big. Four pots you tend beat a 4 by 8 bed you dread.
  • Not thinning. Carrots sown thick and left alone grow into a tangle of pencil-thin roots. Thin to a finger's width apart. It feels wasteful. Do it anyway.
  • Watering by the calendar instead of the soil. Hot, windy days need more; cool, cloudy days need none. Use the finger test.
  • Planting warm crops too early. A tomato set out in cold soil just sits there. One set out two weeks later in warm soil overtakes it.

When will you get your first harvest?

Leafy greens come first, in roughly four to six weeks from sowing; roots take two to three months; tomatoes and cucumbers ripen around two to three months after the plants go out. These are typical ranges for each type of vegetable. Your weather, soil and sun will move them.

  • Spinach: baby leaves in about 4 weeks, full leaves at 6 to 7.
  • Kale: small leaves from about 4 weeks; the plant keeps giving all season.
  • Beets: leaves to eat from 4 weeks, roots at 7 to 9 weeks.
  • Carrots: 9 to 11 weeks, sometimes longer. They are the test of patience in a first garden.
  • Cucumbers: first fruit about 8 to 9 weeks after direct sowing, then a flood.
  • Tomatoes: 10 to 12 weeks after transplanting for a mid-season slicer, and the first ripe one rarely makes it to the kitchen.

Nobody can promise you a harvest. Weather, slugs and a forgotten week in August all get a vote. What you can do is stack the odds: sun, small, thin, water by feel, and pick greens early and often. Do that and a first garden tends to repay you by midsummer.

Common questions

How much space do you need for a vegetable garden?

A 1.2 m by 1.2 m (4 ft by 4 ft) bed or four or five large pots is enough for a first year and will grow a tomato, a cucumber, greens and a short row of roots. Add space next year once you know what you eat and what you actually tend.

What is the easiest vegetable to grow for a beginner?

Leafy greens like kale and spinach are the easiest: they sprout in cool soil, grow fast and you can pick leaves within about a month. Kale 'Red Russian' is especially forgiving because it handles both summer heat and light frost.

Can you start a vegetable garden without a yard?

Yes. A balcony, patio or windowsill with six hours of sun will grow greens, roots and a cucumber on a trellis in large containers. A tomato works too in a pot of 20 litres (5 gallons) or bigger.

What month should I start a vegetable garden?

Count from your last spring frost date rather than the calendar: start tomatoes indoors six to eight weeks before it, sow spinach, kale, carrots and beets outside two to four weeks before it, and plant tomatoes and cucumbers out one to two weeks after it. In most of the US that means sowing between March and May.

Is it cheaper to grow your own vegetables?

For greens, salad and tomatoes, usually yes from the second year on, since a packet of seed costs a few dollars and pots and beds are reused. The first year costs more because of the setup, which is one more reason to start small.
