Fall and Winter Greens on a Balcony: What Survives the Cold

By Happy Seeds · November 25, 2026 · 6 min read

A balcony garden does not have to end at the first frost. The hardy greens carry on, if you protect the pots rather than the plants.

The short answer Kale and turnips keep going in balcony pots through frosts below 28 degrees F, spinach usually does, and beets and carrots tolerate light frosts to about 28 degrees F. Pot roots freeze sooner than garden roots, so group the pots against the building, insulate them and cover the leaves on the coldest nights.

This guide is for the apartment with a balcony, terrace or roof deck the building allows you to use, in a place where fall brings frost. The constraint is cold, and it is harder on a balcony than in a garden: small pots, more wind, and no soil around the roots.

The answer is to grow the right greens and protect the containers, not to haul everything indoors. Kale, spinach and turnips can keep producing into winter, and beets, carrots and chard hold through the first light frosts. The warm-season crops come down the week the forecast first shows a frost.

Which greens survive frost in a balcony pot?

Kale, spinach, turnips, collards and arugula survive heavy frost below 28 degrees F. Beets, carrots, lettuce and chard tolerate only light frosts of 28 to 32 degrees F. Those groupings come from Oregon State Extension's guide to cold-hardy vegetables, which adds that cold does not kill hardy vegetables, it only slows them.

South Dakota State Extension's frost tolerance list puts kale and turnips with the crops that take moderate freezes of 24 to 28 degrees F. It places spinach, beets, carrots and chard with the crops that take light freezes of 28 to 32 degrees F. The two sources differ on spinach, so cover its leaves on frosty nights and count on the roots only into the high twenties.

Why is a balcony pot colder than a garden bed?

A pot is colder because the roots sit in a small volume of mix with cold air on every side. Michigan State Extension notes that root crops are insulated from light frosts only by the ground around them. University of Illinois Extension's guide to overwintering potted plants says pot mix gets much colder than garden soil, and suggests plants hardy to two zones colder than yours.

A kale plant that shrugs off the mid-twenties in a bed can lose its roots at the same temperature in a small pot. Wind makes it worse, because moving air strips heat from the pot walls as well as the leaves. The series post on high-rise balcony gardening covers why a high floor is colder than the street.

How do you protect the pots, not just the plants?

Protect the pots by grouping them against the building wall, wrapping them, and keeping the mix moist, because that is where the cold does its damage. Oklahoma State Extension's guide to fall and winter container gardens recommends a pot of 10 to 12 inches or larger. A pot that size is slower to dry out and slower to freeze solid.

  1. Pull every pot back against the wall of the building, out of the wind, and push them together so they shield each other.
  2. Wrap each pot in bubble wrap or burlap, or set a plastic pot inside a larger one with leaves or crumpled newspaper between.
  3. Water when the top inch of mix is dry, and water the afternoon before a hard frost, because moist mix holds heat and freezes more slowly than dry mix.
  4. Keep a piece of row cover or an old sheet by the door to drape over the leaves on cold nights. Michigan State says plastic sheeting and tarps are not effective covers.
  5. Move clay and terracotta pots out of the freeze if you can, because wet clay cracks; plastic, fabric and wood planters take winter better.

When should you bring the pots in?

Bring hardy greens into a heated apartment only for a night or two of severe cold, not for the season, because a warm room is the wrong climate for them. Oklahoma State advises sheltering pots during cold snaps below 28 degrees F in an unheated garage, shed or covered porch. It says buffering short dips beats shuttling plants between a warm house and the cold.

An apartment has no garage, so for a forecast of the teens use a cool room by the window, away from the radiator. The series post on radiators and indoor seedlings finds that spot. Never stage pots on a fire escape, which is an emergency exit, and keep them out of shared hallways unless the building allows it.

For the light-frost crops, the better answer is to harvest, and beets and carrots should come out before the mix freezes solid. Michigan State notes that lettuce and spinach leaves turn to mush once frozen, so cover them when the forecast drops below 36 degrees F. Kale and turnip greens are the ones worth nursing to midwinter, and both get sweeter after frost.

What does a cold-season balcony look like week to week?

A cold-season balcony is sown in late summer, picked through fall, and protected rather than planted in winter. The packet timing is the plan: kale, beets and turnips 8 to 10 weeks before the first fall frost, spinach 6 to 8, carrots 10 to 12. Confirm that frost date with your county extension office, or start with the state planting calendars.

What about the lease, the weight and the neighbors?

Check your lease and building rules before the winter setup, because wrapped pots and a sheet flapping in January are more visible than summer tomatoes. The series post on lease and building rules covers what landlords usually object to, and the one on balcony weight limits has the numbers for big wet pots. Tie covers down, because a sheet that blows off a balcony lands on someone below.

Common questions

What temperature kills kale in a pot?

Kale survives heavy frost below 28 degrees F in the ground, according to Oregon State Extension, and SDSU puts it in the group that tolerates 24 to 28 degrees F. In a pot the roots are the weak point, so wrap the container and group it against the building when nights fall into the twenties.

Can spinach survive winter on a balcony?

Spinach is one of the hardiest greens, listed by Oregon State among vegetables that take frost below 28 degrees F, though the leaves can be damaged when they freeze. Cover the plants on nights below 36 degrees F and insulate the pot. Growth slows to almost nothing in deep winter and picks up again in late winter.

Should I bring balcony vegetables inside for the winter?

Not into a heated room if they are hardy greens. Oklahoma State Extension says it is better to shelter pots from brief cold snaps than to move plants between a warm apartment and the cold. Pull pots against the wall, wrap them, cover the leaves, and bring them into a cool room only for a severe night.

Why do plants in pots freeze before plants in the ground?

A pot holds a small volume of mix with cold air on every side, so the roots get far colder than roots in garden soil. University of Illinois Extension says that is why potted plants should be hardier than your zone. Moist mix freezes more slowly than dry mix, so do not let winter pots dry out.

When should I plant fall greens for a balcony?

Count back from your first fall frost: kale, beets and turnips 8 to 10 weeks before it, spinach 6 to 8 weeks before, carrots 10 to 12 weeks before. That is late summer in most of the Northeast and Midwest. Confirm the date with your county extension office or the state planting pages on this site.

Does the whole vegetable kit grow on a balcony in winter?

No. The five cool-season packets, kale, spinach, carrot, beet and turnip, are the ones that handle a cold balcony. Tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant and melon are warm-season crops that a light frost kills, so they belong to the summer balcony and come down at the first frost.
