Seed basics

How to read a seed packet, front and back.

By Happy Seeds · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

For anyone standing in a garden aisle wondering what 85% germination, non-GMO or open-pollinated actually means, and what to do with the numbers once you know.

The short answerA seed packet tells you five things: what the variety is, how many seeds will probably sprout (the germination rate), how it was bred (open-pollinated, hybrid or heirloom), when it was tested (the packed-for date and lot number), and how to plant it (depth, spacing, sun and days to maturity). Read the germination rate first. An 85% rate means about 17 of every 20 seeds sprouted in a lab test, so sow two or three per spot and thin the extras. Non-GMO is a transparency label rather than a safety one, because GMO seed is not sold to home gardeners in the first place.

What is on a seed packet, and where should you start?

The front sells the vegetable; the back tells you how to grow it, so flip the packet over before you read anything else. Most packets carry the same handful of facts, and once you know the list you can read any brand in twenty seconds.

  1. Variety name. Not just "tomato" but the cultivar, like Tomato 'Floradade' or Kale 'Red Russian'. The cultivar is what tells you size, flavor and timing. A packet that only says "tomato" is a packet to put back.
  2. Germination rate, or a packed-for date. One of these is required in the US. More on both below.
  3. Lot number. A code that ties your packet to one seed batch and one lab test.
  4. Labels. Non-GMO, organic, open-pollinated, heirloom, hybrid or F1. Each means something specific.
  5. Growing instructions. Planting depth, spacing, sun and days to maturity. The part you will come back to in April.

What does germination rate mean?

Germination rate is the percentage of seeds from a tested sample that sprouted in a lab, so an 85% rate means about 17 of every 20 seeds came up under near-perfect conditions. Your windowsill is not a lab. Cold soil, dry spells and years in a drawer all pull the real number down, so treat the printed rate as a ceiling, not a promise.

The practical use is deciding how many seeds to drop in each hole. Sow a few extra, then thin to the strongest seedling. That is normal, not wasteful.

If a packet prints no rate, run your own test: ten seeds on a damp paper towel, folded into a zip bag, somewhere warm for a week to ten days. Seven sprouts is 70%. Happy Seeds prints the germination rate on every packet in the Veggie Garden Kit, so you can do the sowing math before you open it.

What do non-GMO, organic and lab tested mean?

Non-GMO means the seed was not genetically engineered, and for a home garden packet that is always true, because GMO seed is not sold to home gardeners at all. Genetically engineered crops exist for a short list of commodity plants, mostly corn, soy, canola, cotton, sugar beet and alfalfa, and they go to farmers under contract, by the bag. So the label is not a warning that other packets are risky. It is a company saying plainly what its seed is: transparency, not safety.

Organic is a different claim. It means the seed crop itself was grown and certified under USDA organic rules, which cover the field, the fertilizer and the pest control used on the parent plants. A seed can be non-GMO without being organic, and nearly all organic seed is also non-GMO. The Happy Seeds packet says non-GMO and open-pollinated. It does not say organic, because it is not certified organic, and a packet should only print what it can back up.

Lab tested means a sample from the batch was germinated at a seed laboratory under standardized rules. That test is where the rate on the packet comes from, and it is a point-in-time measurement, which is why the date matters as much as the number.

What is the difference between open-pollinated, hybrid and heirloom seeds?

Open-pollinated seed comes true to type, so you can save seed from this year's plants and grow the same variety next year; hybrid seed does not; and heirloom is simply an open-pollinated variety with a long history. The words describe how a variety was bred, not how good it is.

Seed-saving notes: tomatoes and peppers mostly self-pollinate, so Tomato 'Floradade' and Pepper 'Anaheim Chili' are easy first projects. Cucumbers and melons cross freely with other varieties of the same species, so Cucumber 'Marketmore 76' needs distance from the neighbor's pickles. Carrots, beets, turnips and kale are biennials: they set seed in their second year, so saving Carrot 'Danvers 126' takes patience.

Every variety in the Happy Seeds kit is open-pollinated, so seed saving is on the table. We do not call them heirloom. Some have been around a long time, but there is no certification behind that word, and we would rather print the term we can stand behind.

What do the packed-for date and lot number tell you?

The packed-for date tells you which season the seed was tested and packaged for, and the lot number tells you which batch it came from. Under US federal seed law, small vegetable packets that meet a minimum germination standard may print a "packed for 2027" or "sell by" date instead of the actual percentage. A packet that shows both a date and a rate is telling you more than it had to.

Treat the date as a freshness stamp, not an expiration date. Seed packed for the current season was tested within the past year; seed from two seasons ago is usually still fine, with a lower real-world rate. The lot number matters if something goes wrong: it lets the company trace your packet back to one harvest and one test. Happy Seeds lab tests every lot before it is packed, and the rate on your packet is the rate from that test.

How do you read planting depth, spacing, days to maturity and sun?

These four lines are the actual instructions; the trick is knowing which clock and which measurement each one refers to.

Planting depth

How deep to bury the seed. The rule of thumb is two to three times the seed's width. Big seeds like cucumber and melon go about half an inch to an inch down. Tiny seeds like carrot and kale are barely covered, a quarter inch or less. Planting a small seed too deep is the most common reason it never shows up. Carrots are slow and fussy about this. That's normal.

Spacing

Packets usually give two or three numbers: how far apart to sow, how far apart to thin to, and how far apart to keep rows. "Sow 1 inch apart, thin to 4 inches, rows 12 inches" means drop seeds close, then pull the weakest once they are up so each survivor gets four inches. In a container, ignore the row figure and use the thinning figure in every direction.

Days to maturity

The rough number of days until first harvest, but the clock starts at different points. For crops you direct sow, like carrots, beets, turnips, spinach and kale, it counts from the day the seed goes in the ground. For crops you usually transplant, like tomatoes, peppers and eggplant, it counts from the day the seedling goes outside. A slicing tomato that lists 70 days means 70 days after transplant, which can be 120 days after you started it under a light. Our post on when to start seeds indoors walks through that math. What no packet can tell you is when those days start where you live, because sowing and transplant dates count from your frost dates: our planting calendars by state give the typical frost timing to count from, and your county extension office can confirm it.

Sun

"Full sun" means six or more hours of direct light, and fruiting crops like tomato, pepper, cucumber and melon want closer to eight. "Part sun" means four to six hours, which leafy crops like spinach and kale handle well.

How long do seeds last, and how should you store them?

Most vegetable seeds stay viable for two to five years if kept cool, dry and dark. Heat and moisture are what kill seed. A paper packet in a humid garage ages a year every summer; the same packet sealed in a jar in a closet keeps on ticking.

Those are general ranges for each vegetable type, not a guarantee for any one packet. The rate drifts down each year rather than falling off a cliff. To store seed well:

  • Keep packets in an airtight container: a jar, a lidded tin or a zip bag inside a box.
  • Add a desiccant packet or a spoonful of dry rice to absorb moisture.
  • Pick a spot that stays under about 70 degrees year round. A closet beats a shed; the fridge works if the container is sealed.
  • Write the year on anything you open, and paper-towel test anything older than three years before you count on it.

Opened packets are fine: fold the top over and put it back in the jar. Extra seed is a reason to plant a second bed, not a reason to worry.

Common questions

Can you plant seeds that are past their packed-for date?

Yes. The packed-for date is a freshness stamp, not an expiration date. Most vegetable seed stays viable for two to five years in cool, dry storage; just sow a little thicker to make up for a lower germination rate.

What is the difference between organic and non-GMO seeds?

Non-GMO means the seed was not genetically engineered, which is true of every home garden packet. Organic means the seed crop was grown and certified under USDA organic rules. A seed can be non-GMO without being organic.

Can I save seeds from hybrid tomatoes?

You can, but the plants will not match the parent. Hybrid (F1) seed only holds its trait mix for one generation, so the saved seed grows a mix of the two parent lines. Save seed from open-pollinated varieties instead.

How do I test old seeds for germination at home?

Place ten seeds on a damp paper towel, fold it into a sealed bag, and keep it somewhere warm for seven to ten days. Count the sprouts: seven out of ten means roughly 70% germination, so sow extra to cover the gap.

Why did my seeds not germinate when the packet said 90%?

The printed rate comes from a lab test under ideal warmth and moisture. Cold soil, seed planted too deep, or a surface that dried out between waterings are the usual culprits at home. Check soil temperature and depth first.
