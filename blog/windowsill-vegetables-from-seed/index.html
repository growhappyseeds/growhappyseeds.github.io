5 Vegetables You Can Grow From Seed on an Apartment Windowsill

By Happy Seeds · November 14, 2026 · 7 min read

Which five vegetables are worth a windowsill, how deep a pot each one needs, when to cut, and why the tomato packet waits for a balcony.

The short answer Kale, spinach, beet greens, turnip greens and carrots grow from seed on a windowsill that gets several hours of direct sun. Greens are ready as baby leaves in three to six weeks; carrots need a pot a foot deep and about ten weeks. Tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers are not windowsill crops.

This guide is for the apartment with a decent window and no balcony: no patio, no roof access, and a fire escape does not count. The constraint is light and root room, because a sill gets a few hours of direct sun and holds a pot under a foot deep. Five vegetables are happy inside those limits.

They are kale, spinach, beets grown for their greens, turnip greens and carrots in a deep pot. Each gets a section below with pot depth, sowing and harvest timing, and the last section explains why tomatoes are not on the list.

What vegetables can you grow from seed on a windowsill?

The vegetables that grow from seed on a windowsill are leafy greens and small roots, because they tolerate modest light and fit in a shallow pot. Illinois Extension's list of leafy greens for winter growing includes kale, spinach, turnip greens and beet greens, all seeded straight into containers of potting mix. Carrots join only when the pot is deep enough.

The Old Farmer's Almanac's guide to vegetables you can grow indoors draws the same line. Greens get by on four or more hours of sun a day; fruiting and root crops want six to eight and usually need a lamp. That difference is why the windowsill list is short.

How much light does a windowsill need for vegetables?

A windowsill grows vegetables when it gets several hours of direct sun, and a south or west window is the one most likely to deliver it. Vermont Extension's guide to growing greens in winter suggests 10 to 12 hours of light a day and a spot right in front of a south or west window. It adds a lamp when the sun falls short, and keeps pots away from the hot draft of a radiator.

If seedlings stretch toward the glass and turn pale, the window is not enough on its own. The series post on which window direction is best for vegetables covers how to judge your exposure. The one on grow lights for apartment seedlings covers a basic lamp on a timer.

Kale: Red Russian on the sill

Kale is the most forgiving of the five, because it tolerates lower light, cooler rooms and repeated cutting. Red Russian has flat, purple-veined leaves that are tender when young. Sow seeds a quarter inch deep in a pot 6 to 8 inches deep, then thin to roughly one plant every 4 inches once the seedlings have true leaves.

Cut the lowest leaves first and leave the growing tip alone, the method Illinois Extension recommends for kale harvested repeatedly. When the stems get woody and the leaves small, sow a fresh pot.

Spinach: Bloomsdale without the bolting

Spinach grows well in a window because it likes exactly what an apartment sill offers in the cool months: moderate light and a room that never gets hot. Illinois Extension notes that greens grow and taste better in cooler conditions and that spinach germinates best in cool soil. Keep the pot away from a radiator and it is far less likely to bolt than in a May garden, where heat and long days push it to seed.

Sow Bloomsdale a half inch deep, an inch apart, in a pot at least 6 inches deep, and thin to roughly 3 inches once the seedlings are up. Cut outer leaves as baby spinach from about three weeks on. A pot gives two or three cuttings, so sow another every three weeks.

Beets: greens first, roots if the light allows

Beets earn a place on the sill for their leaves, which are ready weeks before any root forms and taste like a milder chard.

Maryland Extension's guide to growing microgreens and baby greens indoors lists beets among its indoor greens. Its timeline puts baby leaves at 4 to 6 inches tall by day 21 to 40. A 6-inch pot is plenty for greens.

Every Chioggia "seed" is a cluster that sends up several seedlings, so thin to the strongest one roughly every 3 inches. If your window gives six or more hours of direct sun, leave a few plants in a 10-inch pot for small striped roots at 50 to 60 days. In a dimmer window, harvest the greens and be satisfied.

Turnip greens: the fastest leaf on the list

Turnip greens are the quickest of the five to a usable harvest, with peppery leaves ready to cut about four to six weeks after sowing. Purple Top White Globe is grown for its tops as well as its roots, and the tops are the part a windowsill can deliver. Sow a quarter inch deep in a 6-inch pot and thin to roughly 2 or 3 inches apart.

Cut leaves when they are hand-sized and the plants keep producing for several weeks. Roots need a deeper pot, roughly 8 inches, and the same strong sun beets do, so treat them as a bonus.

Carrots: the one that needs a deep pot and patience

Carrots grow on a windowsill only in a pot 12 inches deep, because Danvers 126 sends its root straight down and stops where the pot does.

Gardening Know How's guide to growing carrots indoors puts standard-length carrots at 10 to 12 inches of soil in a sunny window. A tall, narrow pot on the floor under the sill works as well as one on it.

Carrots are the one root crop here and want the brightest spot you have, six or more hours of direct sun.

Scatter seed thinly on the surface, cover with a quarter inch of mix and keep it moist for the one to three weeks carrots take to sprout. Thin to about an inch apart, then to 2 inches once the tops are a few inches tall. Harvest from about 65 to 75 days by pulling one carrot straight up at a time.

Why tomatoes are not a windowsill vegetable

A full-size tomato is not a windowsill crop because it needs direct sun for most of the day, a 5-gallon container and several feet of height. A sill supplies none of the three.

The Almanac's indoor guide rates even dwarf tomatoes as high-light plants in a large, deep pot. Floradade, the variety in the kit, is a full-size tomato that yields 75 to 80 days after transplant and wants real outdoor sun.

The same goes for the other warm-season packets: cucumber wants a 5-gallon pot and a trellis, pepper 3 to 5 gallons, eggplant 5 gallons and melon 10 gallons or more. The window is their nursery, not their home.

Sow tomato 6 to 8 weeks and pepper and eggplant 8 weeks before your last spring frost, and confirm that date with your county extension office.

After that the plant needs a balcony whose lease and building rules allow planters, a roof the building has approved in writing, or a community garden plot, never a fire escape. The post on when to start seeds indoors covers the indoor weeks, and the state planting calendars give the frost window to count from.

Common questions

What vegetables grow best on a windowsill?

Leafy greens and small roots: kale, spinach, beet greens, turnip greens, lettuce, arugula and short carrots in a deep pot. They tolerate the limited light of a window and fit in pots under a foot deep. Fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers and cucumbers need far more sun, space and root room than a sill provides.

How many hours of sun does a windowsill vegetable garden need?

The Almanac's indoor guide puts greens at four or more hours of sun a day, and they grow faster with six. Vermont Extension suggests 10 to 12 hours of light daily in winter, which usually means a south or west window plus a lamp. If seedlings stretch and pale, the window is not bright enough on its own.

How deep does a pot need to be for windowsill vegetables?

Baby greens grow in 2 to 3 inches of potting mix in a tray. Kale, spinach and turnip greens cut as mature leaves do better in a pot 6 to 8 inches deep. Beets grown for roots want about 10 inches, and a standard carrot like Danvers wants 12 inches so the root can run straight down.

Can you grow tomatoes on a windowsill?

Not a full-size tomato. It needs a 5-gallon or larger container, direct sun for most of the day and several feet of height, and a window gives it none of those. Start tomato seeds in the window 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost, then move the plant to a balcony, an approved roof or a community plot.

How long until you can harvest greens grown on a windowsill?

Baby leaves are ready 21 to 40 days after sowing, according to Maryland Extension's indoor greens timeline, and full-size leaves at the 40 to 60 days printed for most greens. Cut the outer leaves and leave the center so the plant keeps producing, and a single pot usually gives one or two more harvests before it is spent.

Can you grow windowsill vegetables in winter?

Yes, with enough light. The room is warm enough year-round, but a winter window gives fewer hours of weaker sun, so greens grow slowly and lean toward the glass. Add a lamp on a timer to reach the 10 to 12 hours of light a day Vermont Extension suggests, and sow every three weeks.
