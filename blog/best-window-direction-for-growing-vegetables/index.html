Which Window Direction Is Best for Growing Vegetables Indoors?

By Happy Seeds · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

What a south, east, west and north window can each grow from seed, how to check the light your sill really gets, and how to get more of it.

The short answer An unobstructed south-facing window is the best window for growing vegetables indoors, because it gets the brightest light for the longest part of the day. East and west windows grow baby greens more slowly, a north window needs a grow light, and tomatoes and other fruiting crops only start at a window.

This guide is for the apartment gardener with no balcony, whose whole garden is a windowsill. In that apartment the limit is light, and the direction the window faces decides how much of it you get.

Here is what a south, east, west and north window can each grow from seed, and how to check what your own window really receives. For containers, soil and a 30-day sowing plan, start with how to grow vegetables from seed in an apartment with no balcony.

Which window direction is best for growing vegetables indoors?

A south-facing window with nothing blocking it is the best window for growing vegetables indoors. University of Missouri Extension's guide to lighting indoor houseplants says windows facing south provide the brightest light for the longest duration. That holds across the United States, and the advantage is biggest from fall to spring, when the sun is low.

The University of Maryland Extension's page on lighting for indoor plants sorts the four directions the same way. Its classes were written for houseplants and are given in foot-candles, a measure of brightness. The last column is our own reading of what each window can grow.

Window faces Direct sun Maryland Extension's light class What we would sow there
South Around the middle of the day High, 500 to 1,000 foot-candles; more with 4 to 6 hours of direct sun Baby kale and spinach, beet and turnip greens, carrots in a deep pot; seedlings bound for outdoors
West Afternoon Medium-bright, 100 to 500; some west windows rate high Baby greens, more slowly than south; watch summer heat at the glass
East Morning Medium-bright, 100 to 500 Baby greens, more slowly than south; the sill stays cooler
North Little or none Low, 25 to 100 Nothing worth cutting without a grow light

Treat the numbers as rough. Illinois Extension's article on houseplant light in winter gives lower figures: about 300 foot-candles for a south window and 150 for east or west. The order is the same in all three sources.

What can you grow in a south-facing window?

A clear south-facing window grows baby greens from seed and, more slowly, carrots, and it is the best place to start seedlings for outdoors. Illinois Extension's guide to growing herbs and lettuce indoors says those crops need 6 to 8 hours of bright light a day. It calls southern exposure windows great.

Kale, spinach, beet greens and turnip greens, cut young, should manage on the same light. Carrots need a pot 12 inches deep, so stand it on a table or plant stand tight against the glass.

A south window still will not carry a full-size tomato to harvest. The glass lets in a few hours of direct sun from one side, and a fruiting plant wants sun for most of the day. For those crops the window is only a nursery.

Is an east- or west-facing window enough for vegetables?

An east- or west-facing window is enough for baby greens, though they grow more slowly than in a south window. Missouri Extension rates east and west windows as well suited to plants in the medium light range. East gets its direct sun in the morning and west in the afternoon.

The Illinois winter-light article offers a useful comparison: an east or west window is like standing about 5 feet back from a south one. Plan on smaller, slower harvests, and cut the leaves young. A pot of baby leaves is a realistic crop in medium light; a full-size kale plant is not.

West can rate a little brighter, and it runs hotter. Maryland Extension lists some west-facing windows in its high-light class, and afternoon sun arrives when the room is already warm. Illinois Extension's lettuce guide says room temperatures of 70 degrees F and below are best, so in a hot summer we would give spinach the east sill.

Can you grow vegetables in a north-facing window?

Not from the window alone: a north-facing window gets little or no direct sun, and that is too little for vegetables. Missouri Extension says north windows are satisfactory only for plants requiring the lower light levels. Seedlings raised there stretch toward the glass and flop.

A north sill still works as the place where a grow light sits. Missouri Extension suggests 12 to 14 hours of lamp light a day where a window adds some light, and more where it adds none. Maryland Extension caps the total at 16 hours a day, because plants need a period of darkness.

Missouri's distance for fluorescent tubes is 6 to 12 inches above the plants. LED lamps vary, so follow the maker's instructions.

How do you tell which way your window faces?

Open the compass app on your phone at the window or find the building on a map app, then watch when direct sun actually lands on the sill. The second check matters more in a city, where the compass can say south and the building across the street says otherwise.

What you see on a clear day Treat the window as
Direct sun on the sill from mid-morning to mid-afternoon South
Direct sun in the afternoon only West
Direct sun in the morning only East
No direct sun, or a building or tree in the way all day North

Missouri Extension says to weigh a window's size, overhang, and shade from trees or buildings, and that the best windows are not shaded by any of them. Check again when the season changes. The winter sun is lower, so sun that clears the rooftop opposite in June can drop behind it in December.

Do not trust your eyes for brightness. Missouri Extension notes that the human eye is a poor judge of light intensity because it adjusts automatically. The plants are the better meter: Illinois Extension's post on houseplants and indoor lighting says plants that stretch or look spindly are short of light.

How do you get more light from the window you have?

Put the pots as close to the glass as they will sit without the leaves touching it, because light falls off quickly inside a room. Maryland Extension says light intensity decreases rapidly with increasing distance. Skip a sill that sits right above a hot radiator.

The glass itself matters. Maryland Extension lists curtains, insect screening, the season, shade from trees and buildings, and the cleanliness and type of window among the things that change light intensity. Wash the inside of the pane, tie back the curtain, and lift the insect screen in the cold months if your lease and window allow it.

Maryland Extension also notes that reflective, light-colored surfaces raise the light level in a room. A sheet of white card behind the pots bounces light onto their shaded side. Illinois Extension's lettuce guide says to rotate containers every few days.

Expect less in winter, when the same houseplant lighting post says light intensity is reduced and supplemental lighting may be needed. Keep the windowsill pots inside the window. Never move them onto a fire escape to chase the sun, because it is an emergency exit and has to stay clear.

Which window is best for starting tomato, pepper and eggplant seeds?

The brightest south-facing window you have is the one for starting warm-season seedlings, and a grow light above them is better still. Missouri Extension's advice for stocky seedlings under fluorescent tubes is to place them within a few inches of the tubes once germination begins. Seedlings raised on window light alone often grow tall and thin.

Common questions

Is a south-facing window enough to grow vegetables without a grow light?

For baby greens, usually yes, if nothing blocks the sun. Illinois Extension says indoor lettuce and herbs need 6 to 8 hours of bright light a day and calls southern exposure great. Growth slows in midwinter, when a lamp may be needed. Tomatoes, peppers and other fruiting crops still need outdoor sun to bear.

Is an east- or west-facing window better for growing vegetables?

West can rate a little brighter, and east stays cooler. University of Maryland Extension rates both as medium-bright light and puts some west-facing windows in its high-light group. Afternoon sun heats a west sill in summer, so an east window can be the kinder place for spinach in hot months.

Can vegetables grow in a north-facing window?

Not from window light alone. A north-facing window gets little or no direct sun, and University of Missouri Extension rates it as satisfactory only for plants that need lower light levels. A grow light close above the pots, run on a timer for most of the day, makes baby greens possible there.

How far from the window can vegetable pots sit?

As close to the glass as they fit without the leaves touching it. University of Maryland Extension says light intensity decreases rapidly with distance. Illinois Extension compares an east or west window to standing about 5 feet back from a south one. A pot across the room is in low light, whatever the window faces.

What if a building blocks my south-facing window?

Judge the window by the sun that reaches the sill, not by the compass. If a taller building or a tree leaves you only a few hours of direct sun, treat the window as east or west. If no direct sun arrives at all, treat it as north and add a grow light.

Do curtains, screens or dirty glass reduce the light plants get?

Yes. University of Maryland Extension lists curtains, insect screening, the season, shade from trees and buildings, and the cleanliness and type of window among the things that change light intensity. Wash the inside of the pane and tie back the curtain. Lift the insect screen in the cold months if your lease and window allow it.
