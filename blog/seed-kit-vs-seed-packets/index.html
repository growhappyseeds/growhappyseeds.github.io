Seed Kit vs Seed Packets: Which Is Better for a First Garden?

By Happy Seeds · October 31, 2026 · 6 min read

Seed kit vs individual seed packets for a first garden: an honest look at cost per variety, seed waste, curation, instructions, gifting, and when packets win.

The short answer A seed kit is the better choice for a first garden because it bundles a curated set of beginner crops with instructions, at a lower cost per variety than buying ten packets one by one. Individual packets are better once you know which specific varieties you want, or you are planting a large plot.

Every first-time gardener hits the same fork: buy a boxed seed kit, or build a garden from individual packets off a rack or a catalog. Both can grow a fine first garden. The right answer depends on how much you already know, how much space you have, and whether anyone is giving this as a gift.

This comparison treats the two options fairly, including the cases where packets are clearly the smarter buy.

How a seed kit compares to individual packets

A seed kit wins on curation, instructions, cost per variety and gifting, while individual packets win on choice, seed quantity per crop and flexibility for experienced gardeners. The table lays out the trade-offs for a typical first garden of about ten crops.

Neither column is "better" in the abstract. The question is which trade-offs you are happy to make this year.

Cost per variety, not cost per seed

Cost per variety is the fair way to compare, because a first garden needs ten different crops, not a thousand carrots. A kit priced at $24.99 for ten packets comes to roughly $2.50 per variety, and you pay once.

Buying ten packets individually means ten line items. Prices for single packets vary widely by company and crop, so we will not quote competitor numbers here, but anyone who has filled a cart at a big catalog seed company knows the total climbs quickly once shipping is added.

Cost per seed tells a different story. A full retail packet of lettuce or carrot can contain several hundred seeds, so the per-seed price is tiny. That only matters if you can use them, which brings us to waste.

Most packets hold far more seed than a small garden uses

A standard retail seed packet usually contains far more seed than a raised bed or balcony garden plants in a season, and the surplus often expires before it is used. A single 4-by-8-foot bed might take 30 carrot seeds, a dozen beets, 10 kale plants and 2 or 3 tomatoes.

Leftover seed does keep. Stored cool, dry and dark, most vegetable seed remains viable for 2 to 5 years, as explained in What Does Germination Rate Mean on a Seed Packet?. But many beginners do not store seed well, and a half-used packet of spinach in a hot garage is simply money gone.

Kits sidestep this by sizing packets for a small garden. Across ten crops, 1,000-plus seeds is still more than a first garden needs, but the waste per crop is much smaller.

Curation is the quiet advantage

Curation matters because the hardest part of a first garden is not planting, it is choosing. A rack of 300 packets offers no signal about which 10 will work together in your climate across a season.

A good kit solves that by pairing cool-season crops you sow in early spring (spinach, kale, beets, turnips, carrots) with warm-season crops you plant after frost (tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant, melon). You get a staggered season, early wins from the greens, and the summer fruiting crops that make the garden feel worth it. For the reasoning behind that mix, see What Are the Best Vegetable Seeds for Beginners?

The flip side is that curation removes choice. If you want a specific heirloom tomato or a purple carrot, a kit will not have it.

Instructions: a booklet versus the back of a packet

Instructions are where kits pull furthest ahead, because a packet has room for a few lines and a kit can include a real guide. The back of a packet typically gives depth, spacing, days to maturity and a one-line sowing window.

That is enough for someone who already knows the difference between "direct sow" and "transplant." It is not enough for a first season. A kit guide, such as the 24-page grow guide in the Happy Seeds box, has space to explain timing relative to your frost date, what a seedling should look like at two weeks, and when to give up on a sowing and try again.

If you go the packet route, plan to supplement with a state planting calendar. The planting pages on this site give sowing windows by state, including New Jersey, Texas and California.

Gifting: kits are built for it

Kits make better gifts because they arrive complete, look intentional, and ask nothing of the recipient at the moment of giving. A box with a guide inside can sit under a tree in December and be opened in March with everything the person needs to begin.

Ten loose packets in an envelope, however carefully chosen, read as a project rather than a present. If you are shopping for someone else, The Best Gift for Someone Who Wants to Start a Garden covers what to pair with seeds and what to write in the card.

When individual packets are the better choice

Individual packets are the better choice for experienced gardeners, anyone who wants a specific variety, and anyone planting a large plot. In those cases a kit's curation is a limitation, not a help.

  • You already know what grows well in your yard and want to repeat it.
  • You want a particular variety for flavor, color, disease resistance or a short season.
  • You are planting rows, not a bed, and need hundreds of seeds of one crop.
  • You only need to refill one or two crops to round out seed you already own.

There is also a middle path. Many gardeners start with a kit in year one, learn which crops they actually eat and which thrive in their spot, and then switch to individual packets for the crops they want more of. That is a sensible way to spend a first season without overbuying.

The short version

Choose a kit if this is your first garden, your space is small, or the seeds are a gift. Choose packets if you have gardened before, want specific varieties, or need volume. Either way, buy seed that names its varieties and prints a germination rate, and check your sowing dates against a local frost calendar before anything goes in the ground.

Common questions

Is a seed kit cheaper than buying seed packets?

Usually, per variety. A kit that includes ten packets for $24.99 works out to about $2.50 per packet, and you avoid paying shipping on several small orders or driving to find packets in stock. Per seed, large individual packets can be cheaper, but only if you actually plant that many seeds.

Do seed kits have fewer seeds than regular packets?

Kit packets are often smaller than full retail packets, which suits a small garden. A standard retail packet can hold hundreds of lettuce or carrot seeds, far more than a raised bed or balcony uses in a season. A kit with 1,000 or more seeds across ten crops covers a first garden with seed left over.

Are seed kit varieties any good?

Good kits use the same named, open-pollinated varieties you would find in a catalog, such as Marketmore 76 cucumber or Bloomsdale spinach, and are held to the same US germination standards. Check that the kit names its varieties and prints a germination rate; avoid kits that list only the crop.

When should I buy individual seed packets instead of a kit?

Buy individual packets when you want a specific variety, you are planting a large plot that needs hundreds of seeds of one crop, or you already have a few seasons behind you and know what grows well in your spot. Packets also make sense for refilling one or two crops.

Can I save leftover seeds from a kit or packet for next year?

Yes. Store opened packets sealed in a jar in a cool, dry, dark place. Most vegetable seed stays viable for 2 to 5 years, though onion, parsnip and some lettuce decline faster. Do a quick paper-towel test before planting older seed so you know how thickly to sow.
