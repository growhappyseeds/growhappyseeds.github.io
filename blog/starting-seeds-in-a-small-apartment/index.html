Where to Start Seeds in a Small Apartment: Warm Spots That Work

By Happy Seeds · November 18, 2026 · 6 min read

Seeds want warmth first and light second, which means the best place to start them in a small apartment is rarely the windowsill.

The short answer Start seeds wherever your apartment stays steadily warm, since seeds do not need light until they sprout: the top of the fridge, a shelf near a radiator, or a counter away from the window. Move the tray to your brightest window or a lamp the day the first sprouts appear.

This guide is for anyone starting vegetable seeds inside a small city apartment, whether or not there is a balcony at the end of the season. The constraint is space and heat: a studio or one-bedroom has one or two windows, a radiator under each, and not much flat surface. The good news is that seeds do not care about the window until they sprout.

Think of seed starting as two jobs in two places. Germination needs warmth and moisture, which a shelf or the top of the fridge can supply. Growing needs light, which only a window or a lamp can supply, so the tray moves on the day the first sprouts show.

Do seeds need light to germinate?

Most vegetable seeds do not need light to germinate, which is why the germination tray can live away from the window. What they need is a steadily warm, evenly moist growing mix. University of Minnesota Extension's guide to starting seeds indoors puts it simply: most seeds need consistently warm soil to germinate.

That changes the whole plan for a small apartment. Instead of crowding every tray onto one sunny sill, you keep the sill for seedlings that are already up. The trays still waiting to sprout sit somewhere warm, dark and out of the way.

The same guide makes a point many apartment growers miss: a windowsill can be the coldest place in the house. Glass loses heat all night, and seeds on the sill sit in that cold while the room behind them stays warm.

How warm does the spot need to be?

A potting mix temperature of 65 to 75 degrees F suits most vegetable seeds, according to University of Maryland Extension's page on starting vegetable seeds indoors. A heated apartment in winter is usually inside that range already. The mix itself can run several degrees cooler than the air, which is why a warm surface underneath helps.

Different crops have different sweet spots. Alabama Extension's table of soil temperature conditions for vegetable seed germination shows how wide the gap is between the cool-season and warm-season crops in a ten-packet kit.

Crop Minimum soil temperature Optimal range Best spot in an apartment
Spinach 35 F 45 to 75 F Any room-temperature shelf, even a cool one
Carrot 40 F 45 to 85 F Any room-temperature shelf
Beet 40 F 50 to 85 F Any room-temperature shelf
Turnip 40 F 60 to 105 F A warm shelf or counter
Cucumber 60 F 60 to 95 F Top of the fridge or near a radiator
Pepper 60 F 65 to 95 F Top of the fridge or near a radiator
Eggplant 60 F 75 to 90 F The warmest spot you have
Tomato 50 F 70 to 95 F Top of the fridge or near a radiator
Melon (muskmelon) 60 F 75 to 95 F The warmest spot you have

Kale is not in that table, but its close relative cabbage germinates from 40 F with an optimal range of 45 to 95 F. In practice the five cool-season crops sprout almost anywhere indoors. The five warm-season crops are the ones worth hunting for a warm spot.

Where are the warm spots in a small apartment?

The warmest steady spots in most apartments are the top of the refrigerator, a shelf above or beside a radiator, and a kitchen counter away from the window. University of Maryland Extension names the top of a refrigerator as a warm location that hastens germination. University of New Hampshire Extension's seed starting fact sheet lists radiators among the places growers use to hold ideal germination temperatures.

Spot Why it works Watch out for
Top of the refrigerator Gentle, steady heat from the appliance Out of sight, so the tray dries out; check it daily
Shelf above a radiator Rising warm air keeps the mix warm Too hot when the heat cycles on; keep the tray a foot or more above the radiator
Bookshelf on an interior wall Steady room temperature, no drafts, no cold glass Spills, so use a waterproof tray under every pot
Kitchen counter near the stove Warm when you cook, bright enough to check on Cooking splatter and a counter you need back by dinner
Top of a kitchen cabinet Warm air collects near the ceiling Hard to reach, so only for a tray you can lift down easily

A few spots are wrong even though they look convenient. The windowsill is cold at night, as noted above, and any surface in direct sun will overheat a covered tray. The fire escape is an emergency exit, not a shelf; blocking it breaks fire code in most cities, so it never holds a pot or a tray.

Weight is not the problem indoors that it is on a balcony. A small seed tray weighs a few pounds wet, and a full flat somewhat more, so a bookshelf or the fridge handles it without a second thought. The problem is water, so every tray gets a waterproof saucer or a boot tray under it before it goes on a shelf.

How do you keep a tray moist on a shelf?

Cover the tray loosely with clear plastic until the seeds sprout, then take the cover off the day they appear. University of Maryland Extension suggests clear plastic or a plastic bag over the container, kept from touching the growing medium, and removed as soon as sprouts appear. Iowa State University Extension's guide to starting seed indoors gives the same advice: plastic wrap, a bag or glass to seal in moisture, lifted as soon as the seed germinates.

University of New Hampshire Extension adds two details that matter in an apartment. Keep the plastic an inch or two above the mix, and keep covered trays out of direct sun. The temperature under plastic in sunlight can rise enough to kill the seeds, and a shelf on an interior wall avoids both problems.

Water the mix well before sowing, then mist the surface rather than pouring. Under a cover the mix stays damp for days, so the daily job is a look, not a watering.

When do you move the tray to the light?

Move the tray to the brightest window or under a lamp on the day the first sprouts break the surface. A seedling left on a dark shelf for more than a day or so after sprouting starts to stretch, and a stem that has stretched never shortens again. The post on why apartment seedlings get leggy covers what happens when the move comes late.

Seedlings also want it cooler than seeds did. University of Maryland Extension recommends 55 to 60 degrees F at night and 65 to 70 by day once seedlings are up. Iowa State puts it another way: the best growing temperature for a seedling is about 10 degrees cooler than the best germination temperature.

So the warm shelf hands off to the cool, bright window. If the window is above a radiator, set the tray at the cooler end of the sill, or on a small table pulled up beside the glass.

The post on the best window direction for vegetables covers what each exposure can grow. The post on grow lights for apartment seedlings explains when a window is not enough.

Which seeds in the kit start on a shelf, and where do they end up?

All ten packets in the Happy Seeds Veggie Garden Seed Kit can germinate somewhere warm, but only five of them can finish in a window. Kale, spinach, carrot, beet and turnip are cool-season crops that grow in a sunny window or a balcony planter. Tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant and melon are warm-season crops that need a big container and real sun.

One caution on the shelf method: carrot, beet and turnip are root crops that transplant poorly, so sow them straight into the pot they will grow in. The pot itself can spend its first days on the warm shelf until sprouts show. The kit's guide also has cucumber and melon sown direct once the soil is warm, so the tray method is mainly for tomato, pepper and eggplant.

For a no-balcony apartment, that means sowing the cool-season five for the window.

Start the warm-season five only if they have somewhere to go: a balcony, a roof the building allows, or a community plot. Balcony readers should check the lease, the building rules and the balcony's weight limit before planning big wet containers out there.

Common questions

Do seeds need light to germinate?

Most vegetable seeds do not. They need steady warmth and even moisture, and they sprout in the dark under the soil surface. That is why the germination tray can sit on top of the fridge or on a shelf. Light becomes essential the moment the first sprouts break the surface, so move the tray that day.

Is the top of the refrigerator a good place to start seeds?

Yes, for germination only. University of Maryland Extension lists the top of a refrigerator as a warm location that hastens germination, since the appliance gives off a little steady heat. Check the tray daily, because it is out of sight and dries out easily, and move it to light as soon as seedlings appear.

What temperature do seeds need to germinate?

University of Maryland Extension says a potting mix temperature of 65 to 75 degrees F suits most vegetable seeds. Warm-season crops such as tomato and pepper prefer the top of that range or above, while spinach and lettuce sprout happily in a cooler room. A normal heated apartment is usually inside the range already.

Should I cover the seed tray with plastic?

A loose cover helps the mix stay moist while seeds germinate. University of Maryland Extension suggests clear plastic or a plastic bag that does not touch the growing medium, removed as soon as sprouts appear. Keep a covered tray out of direct sun, because the heat under plastic can climb high enough to kill seeds.

Can I start seeds on a windowsill above a radiator?

You can germinate them there, since the warmth speeds sprouting, but it is a poor spot for seedlings afterward. University of Minnesota Extension notes a windowsill can be the coldest place in the house at night, and a radiator under it makes it one of the hottest by day. That swing stretches seedlings, so move the tray.
