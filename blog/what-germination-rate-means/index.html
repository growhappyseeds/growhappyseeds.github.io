What Does the Germination Rate on a Seed Packet Mean?

By Happy Seeds · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

Germination rate meaning explained: how to read the percentage on a seed packet, what US seed laws require, how to test seeds at home, and how long seeds last.

The short answer Germination rate is the percentage of seeds in a tested sample that sprouted under controlled lab conditions, so a packet marked 85 percent means roughly 85 of every 100 seeds should come up. Use it to decide how thickly to sow: the lower the rate, the more seeds you plant per spot.

That small percentage on the back of a seed packet is the most useful number printed there, and most beginners skip right past it. It tells you how many seeds to plant per spot, whether an old packet is worth sowing, and whether a seed company is being straight with you.

Here is what the number means, where it comes from, and how to check it yourself at the kitchen table.

What germination rate means

Germination rate is the share of seeds that sprouted when a sample from the seed lot was tested under controlled temperature and moisture, expressed as a percentage. If a lab places 400 seeds on moist paper in a warm chamber and 352 produce a normal seedling, the lot's germination rate is 88 percent.

The test measures the seed itself, not your garden. In real soil, with cold snaps, crusting and insects, emergence will be lower than the printed rate. Think of the number as the ceiling, not a promise.

The practical use is simple. At 90 percent, sowing two seeds per spot and thinning to one is plenty. At 65 percent, sow three or four. For transplants, where every cell matters, sow two seeds per cell and snip the weaker one.

Why US seed packets have to meet a minimum

US seed laws set minimum germination standards for vegetable seed, and seed that tests below the standard for its crop must be labeled as substandard or cannot be sold as ordinary seed. The Federal Seed Act and state seed laws also require truthful labeling of the kind and variety, the lot, and the test date or packed-for season.

The minimums vary by crop because some seeds are naturally harder to sprout than others. The standards are set at a level that assumes a reasonable, fresh lot, so a packet that meets them from a reputable source should perform well.

Most companies test above the minimum, and many print their actual tested rate rather than simply meeting the law. Happy Seeds prints the lab-tested germination rate on every packet in its kit, which lets you adjust your sowing density crop by crop instead of guessing.

How to read a seed packet

A seed packet carries five pieces of information that matter: the lot number, the packed-for date, the germination rate, days to maturity, and planting depth. Everything else is nice to have.

Field What it tells you How to use it
Lot number Which batch of seed this is Reference if you have a problem; lots are tracked by the seller
Packed for The season the seed was tested and packaged for Not an expiry date; expect lower germination each year after
Germination % Lab-tested sprouting percentage for the lot Sow more seeds per spot as the rate falls
Days to maturity Days from sowing (or from transplant) to first harvest Check that your season is long enough; see the planting calendars
Planting depth How deep to cover the seed Usually 2 to 3 times the seed's width; shallow for tiny seed

Days to maturity needs one caution. For crops normally transplanted, such as tomatoes and peppers, the number usually counts from transplant, not from sowing, so add the 6 to 8 indoor weeks when planning. The state pages under planting calendars handle that math for you.

Variety name matters too. A packet that says only "Tomato" tells you nothing about size, days or disease resistance; one that says "Floradade" or "Marketmore 76" lets you look up exactly what to expect.

How to do a paper-towel germination test at home

A paper-towel test takes ten seeds, a damp towel, a plastic bag and about a week, and it gives you a usable germination percentage for any packet you own. It is the same principle a seed lab uses, just at kitchen scale.

  1. Count out 10 seeds (20 if you have plenty and want a finer result).
  2. Dampen a paper towel so it is wet but not dripping, and lay the seeds on half of it.
  3. Fold the towel over the seeds, slide it into a zip bag or lidded container, and label it with the crop and date.
  4. Keep it somewhere warm, roughly 70 to 80 degrees F; the top of a refrigerator works.
  5. Check daily, re-wetting the towel if it dries, and remove any seed that molds.
  6. After the expected time, count the sprouted seeds and multiply by 10 for your percentage.

Expected times differ. Radish, lettuce, cucumber and brassicas show roots in 3 to 7 days. Tomatoes, beets and beans take 5 to 10. Peppers, carrots, parsley and spinach can take 10 to 14, so do not call them dead early.

Six or more out of ten is worth sowing, just a little thicker. Below five, buy fresh seed for anything you care about. You can even plant the sprouted test seeds if you handle the roots gently.

How long seeds stay viable, by crop

Most vegetable seeds remain viable for 1 to 5 years, with onions and parsnips at the short end and tomatoes, cucumbers and brassicas at the long end. The ranges below assume cool, dry, dark storage; a hot garage or a humid basement can cut them in half.

Crop Typical viability Notes
Onion, leek, parsnip 1 to 2 years Buy fresh each season if you can
Pepper, spinach, sweet corn 2 to 3 years Test before year three
Carrot, bean, pea 2 to 4 years Carrot germination drops noticeably after year two
Lettuce, beet, turnip, eggplant 3 to 5 years Beet and turnip are reliable keepers
Tomato, cucumber, melon, squash 4 to 5 years Among the longest-lived garden seeds
Kale, cabbage, broccoli, radish 4 to 5 years Store well in a sealed jar

Storage is the variable you control. Fold the packet closed, put it in a sealed jar or tin with a packet of silica gel or a spoonful of dry rice, and keep it in a cool closet or the refrigerator. Seed kept this way will outperform the table; seed left in a shed will not.

Putting the number to work

Use the germination rate to set your sowing density, use a paper-towel test to update it for older seed, and keep the packets you did not finish in a sealed jar for next year. Those three habits remove most of the mystery from "my seeds did not come up."

If you are choosing seed for a first garden, favor packets that name the variety and print a real tested rate; that transparency usually travels with other good practices. Common questions

What is a good germination rate for vegetable seeds?

Anything at or above the federal minimum for that crop is acceptable, and most fresh vegetable seed tests between 80 and 95 percent. Rates in the 70s are fine for direct-sown crops if you sow a little thicker. Below about 50 percent, it is usually cheaper and less frustrating to buy fresh seed.

Does germination rate change over time?

Yes, it falls as seed ages, and the decline speeds up with heat and humidity. The rate on a packet reflects the test done before packing, so a three-year-old packet will usually sprout at a lower percentage than printed. A home paper-towel test tells you where it stands now.

What does 'packed for' mean on a seed packet?

The packed-for date is the season the seed was tested and packaged to be sold in, for example 'Packed for 2026.' It is not an expiration date. Seed packed for a past season is often still good, but its germination rate will have drifted below the printed figure.

How do I test old seeds to see if they are still good?

Place 10 seeds on a damp paper towel, fold it over, seal it in a plastic bag, and keep it somewhere warm for 5 to 14 days depending on the crop. Count the sprouted seeds and multiply by 10 to get a rough percentage. Six or more sprouting means the seed is still usable.

Why do my seeds germinate below the rate on the packet?

Garden conditions are harsher than a lab. Cold soil, sowing too deep, crusted or dry soil, waterlogging and hungry insects all reduce emergence. A packet rate of 90 percent is a ceiling under ideal conditions; 60 to 80 percent emergence outdoors is normal and not a sign of bad seed.
