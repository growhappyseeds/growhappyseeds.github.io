Seed starting

When to start seeds indoors (and how to do it right).

By Happy Seeds · November 1, 2026 · 7 min read

For first-time seed starters who want to know which packets to start under a light in late winter, which to sow straight into the soil, and when.

The short answerStart most seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your area's last spring frost date. Tomatoes, peppers and eggplant need that full head start; cucumbers and melons need only 3 to 4 weeks or can be sown outdoors; carrots, beets, turnips and spinach do best sown directly in the ground. Find your last frost date, count back on a calendar, and write the sowing date on each packet.

What is a last frost date, and how do I find mine?

Your last frost date is the average date of the last spring frost where you live, and it is the anchor every indoor sowing date counts back from. It is an average, not a promise: in a cold spring, frost can arrive a week or two later. Treat it as a planning number.

In the US, the quickest way to find it is to search for your zip code plus "last frost date", or to ask your county extension office. Write it on a calendar. Across much of the northern half of the country it falls somewhere between late April and mid May; in the South and along the West Coast it can be March or earlier.

Two things to keep in mind. First, frost-tender crops like tomatoes and peppers should not go outside on the frost date itself. They want warm soil and warm nights, which usually means one to two weeks after. Second, if your garden sits in a low spot, add a week of caution. Cold air settles.

Most vegetables worth starting indoors want a 3 to 8 week head start, and the table below gives the usual range for each type in the kit. Count back from your last frost date, mark the sowing week on the calendar, and write it on the packet so you never have to work it out twice.

Which seeds should I skip indoors and sow straight in the ground?

Root crops and spinach are better sown where they will grow: carrots, beets and turnips resent having their roots disturbed, and spinach bolts quickly if it is transplanted into warm weather. Carrot 'Danvers 126' in particular throws forked, stubby roots if the taproot is bent during transplanting. Sow them outside, thin them, and leave them alone.

A second group makes indoor starting optional. Cucumber 'Marketmore 76' and Melon 'Honeydew Orange' germinate fast in warm soil and do not love being moved, so many gardeners direct sow them a week or two after the last frost and skip the trays. If your summers are short, a 3 to 4 week head start in a larger pot (so the roots stay intact) buys you time. Kale 'Red Russian' works either way; indoors gets you earlier leaves, outdoors is less fuss.

The third group has no choice. Tomato 'Floradade', Pepper 'Anaheim Chili' and Eggplant 'Black Beauty' are slow, heat-loving and frost-tender. In most of the country the season is not long enough to direct sow them and still see ripe fruit. These three are the real reason to set up a light in February.

If this is your first garden and the indoor setup feels like a lot, it is fine to start only the tomatoes and peppers inside and direct sow everything else. And if you miss the spring window entirely, kale, spinach, carrot, beet and turnip get a second chance in late summer; see fall vegetables to plant in late summer.

What do seedlings need indoors: light, warmth and water?

Seedlings indoors need three things, in order: warmth to germinate, strong light the moment they sprout, and water that keeps the mix damp but never soggy. Get those right and the rest is patience.

Warmth

Most vegetable seeds germinate fastest in soil between 70 and 85°F (21 to 29°C). Peppers and eggplant are the fussiest and can sit for three weeks in a cool room. The top of a refrigerator, a warm cupboard or a seedling heat mat all work. Once seedlings are up, move them somewhere cooler, around 65 to 70°F, so they grow stocky instead of stretched.

Light

A south-facing window in February is not enough for most homes; the days are short and the glass cuts the light. An inexpensive LED shop light or a purpose-made grow light, hung 2 to 4 inches above the leaves and run 14 to 16 hours a day on a timer, is the single biggest upgrade you can make. Raise the light as the plants grow.

Water and containers

Use a seed-starting mix, not garden soil, and pre-moisten it before you fill the cells. Sow at the depth printed on the packet, which for most of these is about a quarter inch, and label everything. Water from below by setting the tray in an inch of water for ten minutes, then let it drain. Run a small fan on low for a few hours a day; the moving air strengthens stems and dries the surface, which matters for the last section.

Pot tomatoes, peppers and eggplant up into 3 to 4 inch pots when roots fill the cell.

How do I harden off seedlings before planting them outside?

Hardening off means getting indoor seedlings used to sun, wind and cool nights over 7 to 10 days, and skipping it is the fastest way to lose plants you spent two months growing. Indoor leaves are soft. Full sun on day one will scorch them white within hours.

  1. Days 1 to 2: set the trays outside in full shade, out of the wind, for 2 to 3 hours. Bring them in.
  2. Days 3 to 4: morning sun for 2 to 3 hours, then shade. Water more often; outdoor air dries pots fast.
  3. Days 5 to 7: half a day of sun, including some breeze.
  4. Days 8 to 10: a full day out, and if nights stay above 50°F (10°C), leave them out overnight.
  5. Plant out on a cloudy, still day or in the evening, and water in well.

Tomatoes, cucumbers and kale harden off fairly quickly. Peppers and eggplant sulk if nights drop below 55°F, so give them the full ten days and an extra week of waiting if the forecast is cool.

Why are my seedlings leggy, and why did they fall over?

Leggy seedlings (long, pale, bending toward the window) are reaching for light that is not strong enough, and seedlings that collapse at the soil line have damping off, a fungal problem that thrives in wet, still, cool conditions. Both are common. Both are mostly preventable.

Leggy seedlings

Move the light closer, run it longer, and lower the room temperature a few degrees. If they are already stretched, tomatoes forgive you: bury the stem up to the first leaves when you pot up and roots will grow along it. Peppers, eggplant, cucumbers and melons do not recover the same way, so for those it is often faster to resow with the light fixed.

Damping off

Once a seedling has toppled with a pinched, brown stem there is no saving that plant. Pull it, improve airflow, and let the surface dry between waterings. To prevent it next time: use fresh sterile mix, wash trays in hot soapy water, water from below, run the fan, and do not sow so thickly that the seedlings shade each other. Sowing two or three seeds per cell and snipping all but the strongest with scissors gives you a backup without the crowding.

Other common misses

  • Nothing came up. Usually too cold or sown too deep. Check the germination rate and depth printed on the packet (how to read a seed packet explains what those numbers mean) and try again with bottom heat.
  • Started too early. A twelve-week-old tomato in a two-inch cell is root-bound and flowering under a shop light. Trust the count-back; a week late beats a month early.
  • Sunburn after planting out. White, papery patches on leaves the day after transplanting mean the hardening off was rushed.

Peppers are slow. Eggplant is slower. That is normal, and it is the whole reason an indoor head start exists.

Common questions

Is it too late to start seeds indoors in April?

Not for cucumbers, melons and kale, which only need 3 to 4 weeks indoors. For tomatoes, peppers and eggplant, April is late in most of the US; you can still sow them, but plan on planting out later and a shorter season.

Can I start seeds indoors without a grow light?

Yes, in a bright south-facing window, but expect leggier plants. Turn the tray a quarter turn every day, keep the room cool, and consider sowing only 4 to 6 weeks before the frost date so seedlings spend less time stretching.

Do seeds need light to germinate?

Most vegetable seeds do not; they need warmth and moisture. Light matters the moment the sprout breaks the surface, so have the light set up before the seedlings emerge.

What is the cheapest way to start seeds indoors?

Recycled yogurt cups or egg cartons with drainage holes punched in the bottom, one bag of seed-starting mix and a basic LED shop light. The light is the one thing worth buying new.

How do I know my seedlings are ready to transplant?

Look for two to three sets of true leaves, a stem that stands on its own, roots that hold the plug together when you lift it, and 7 to 10 days of hardening off behind them.
