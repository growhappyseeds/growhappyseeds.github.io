Why Are My Seeds Not Germinating? 9 Causes and Fixes

By Happy Seeds · November 24, 2026 · 7 min read

An empty tray is almost always a temperature, water or depth problem, not bad seed. Here is how to tell which, and what to do about it.

The short answer Seeds usually fail to germinate because the soil is too cold, too wet or too dry, the seed was buried too deep, or the seed is old. Check the soil temperature and moisture first, then wait out the full germination window for that crop before you resow a little shallower in fresh mix.

Nothing happens. A week after sowing, the tray is bare, and the question is whether to wait, water, dig or start over. The good news is that seeds fail for a short list of reasons, and most of them are about conditions, not the seed itself.

The nine causes below run in rough order of likelihood, each with the sign that points to it and the fix. They apply to a windowsill tray and to a garden bed alike.

Have you waited long enough?

Probably not, if the soil is cool: most vegetable seeds come up in 4 to 14 days in warm soil and several times slower in cool soil. Patience comes before any other fix.

Oregon State University Extension's table of soil temperature conditions for vegetable seed germination gives the days to emergence at each soil temperature. The gap between a cool room and a warm one is large.

Crop 50 F 59 F 68 F 77 F 86 F
Tomato 43 days 14 8 6 6
Pepper little or none 25 12 8 8
Cucumber little or none 13 6 4 3
Carrot 17 10 7 6 6
Beet 17 10 6 5 4
Spinach 12 7 6 5 6
Turnip 5 3 2 1 1

Figures are Oregon State's, for seed planted half an inch deep. A pepper sown in a 59-degree room is on schedule at day 20, not failed. If you are inside the window for your crop and temperature, keep the mix damp and wait.

The nine causes, and the fix for each

The nine causes are cold soil, hot soil, too much water, too little water, planting too deep, old seed, the wrong soil, pests, and the wrong time outdoors. The table gives the sign for each; the sections after it give the fix.

1. The soil is too cold

Cold soil is a very common reason indoor seeds stall, because a windowsill in March is colder than the room. Iowa State University Extension's answer to why seeds started indoors germinate poorly says most seeds germinate best with the medium at 70 to 75 degrees F. Oregon State lists a minimum of 60 degrees for pepper, eggplant, cucumber and melon.

Move the tray to the top of the refrigerator or another warm spot, or use a heat mat, until the seedlings emerge. Then move them straight into bright light. A cheap soil thermometer settles the question in a minute.

2. The soil is too hot

Hot soil stops cool-season seeds, and spinach is the classic case. Oregon State gives spinach a maximum of 85 degrees F, with little or no germination at 95. A sunny July windowsill or a black tray in full sun can reach that.

Sow spinach, lettuce and the other cool crops in a cooler room or a shaded spot, or wait for the season to turn. The warm-season crops have the opposite problem, so keep the two groups in different places.

3. Too much water

Waterlogged mix drowns and rots seed before it sprouts. Penn State Extension's guide to damping off describes pre-emergence damping off.

Fungi and water molds such as Pythium and Rhizoctonia destroy the root and shoot before they reach the surface. Cold and wet together are the worst combination.

Start again in fresh, sterile seed-starting mix in a container with drainage holes. Water from below by setting the tray in an inch of water for a few minutes, then let it drain. Iowa State suggests covering the tray with plastic until germination, then removing it.

4. Too little water

A seed that dries out after it has started to swell usually dies, and a dry crust on the surface hides a dry tray underneath. Iowa State calls for uniform moisture through the whole germination period. Fine seeds on the surface dry fastest.

Wet the mix thoroughly before you sow, cover the tray loosely with plastic or a clear lid, and check daily. Remove the cover the day the first sprouts appear so the seedlings do not stretch or rot.

5. Planted too deep

A seed buried too deep runs out of stored energy before the shoot reaches light. Iowa State's rule is to cover medium and large seeds to one to two times their diameter, and to press fine seeds onto the surface. The full chart for ten crops is in how deep to plant vegetable seeds.

If big seeds came up and small ones did not, depth is the likely cause. Resow the small ones shallower, with a dusting of mix, and keep the surface damp.

6. Old or badly stored seed

Seed stored warm or damp loses viability, and Iowa State says viability drops significantly after two to three years. If every condition above is right and the count is still low, the seed is the suspect. Do seeds expire? has the shelf life by crop.

Test before you blame: ten seeds on a damp paper towel in a sealed bag in a warm spot. The method is in what the germination rate on a seed packet means. Sow thicker with weak seed, or buy a fresh packet.

7. The wrong soil

Garden soil in a tray compacts, crusts and holds too much water, and a hard crust can hold a seedling under. Use a seed-starting or potting mix indoors, and outdoors rake the bed fine and cover the row with mix rather than clods.

If a crust has formed, mist it until it softens and break it gently with a fork. A thin layer of vermiculite over the row keeps the surface loose.

8. Something ate or moved the seed

Missing seeds, small holes in the mix and clipped stems point to animals. Burpee's guide to why seeds did not sprout lists birds, squirrels, voles and, indoors, mice. Heavy watering can also wash small seeds out of a tray.

Outdoors, lay floating row cover or mesh over the bed until seedlings are up. Indoors, keep trays off the floor and water gently.

9. Sown at the wrong time outdoors

Warm-season seeds sown in cold spring soil rot or sit for weeks, and a late frost kills anything that did come up. Burpee notes that tomatoes and cucumbers want nights above 55 degrees F before they go out. Soil temperature matters more than the calendar.

Check your last frost date with your county extension office and use the state planting calendars for the sowing window for each crop. The guide to when to start seeds indoors covers counting back from that date.

How do you restart after a failed sowing?

Restart with fresh mix, a warm spot, shallow sowing and a cover, and most second attempts succeed. Empty the failed cells, since old wet mix may carry the fungi that killed the first batch. Sow two or three seeds per cell and thin later.

Set a soil thermometer in the tray and look at it, not the room thermostat. The moment sprouts appear, remove the cover and move the tray to your brightest window or under a lamp, or the seedlings will stretch; leggy seedlings explains why.

Common questions

How long should I wait before giving up on seeds?

Wait for the longest figure in the crop's germination window plus a few days, and longer in cool soil. Oregon State's table shows tomato taking 6 days at 77 degrees F but 43 days at 50, and carrot 6 days at 77 but 17 at 50. Peppers and carrots are the usual slow ones.

Can seeds rot before they sprout?

Yes. In cold, soggy mix, fungi and water molds such as Pythium attack the seed and the emerging root before the shoot reaches the surface, which Penn State Extension calls pre-emergence damping off. The cell stays empty and the seed, if you dig it up, is soft and brown. Resow in fresh mix kept damp, not wet.

Do seeds need light to germinate?

A few do, mostly fine-seeded crops like lettuce, and those go on the surface and are pressed in rather than covered. Most vegetable seeds do not need light and germinate under a thin layer of mix. All seedlings need strong light the moment they emerge, so have a sunny window or lamp ready before they come up.

Will old seeds still germinate?

Often, at a lower rate. Iowa State Extension says viability drops significantly after two to three years, so a packet that gave nine sprouts in ten when new may give five or six later. Test old seed on a damp paper towel before you plant it, and sow thicker to make up the difference.

Should I dig up seeds that have not come up?

Dig up one, gently, with a spoon. A firm seed that has swelled is alive and just slow, so put it back and wait. A soft, brown or moldy seed has rotted, usually from cold wet mix. A seed that is not there was eaten or washed out. Each answer points to a different fix.

Is it the seed or is it me?

Run a paper towel test: ten seeds on a damp towel in a sealed bag somewhere warm. If most sprout in a week or two, the seed is fine and the problem is in the pot, usually temperature, water or depth. If few sprout, the seed is old or was stored badly, and you need a fresh packet.
