How to Grow Vegetables From Seed in an Apartment, No Balcony

By Happy Seeds · November 4, 2026 · 7 min read

Which crops grow in a window, how to judge your light, where to put the pots, and a 30-day plan from packet to first harvest.

The short answer Leafy greens and small root crops grow from seed in an apartment with no balcony, given one window with six or more hours of bright light or a grow light. Kale, spinach, beet greens, turnip greens and carrots in a deep pot work; tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplant and melons need a big container and real sun outdoors.

This guide is for the apartment with a window and nothing else: no balcony, no patio, no roof access, maybe a landlord who frowns at drips. You can still grow real vegetables from seed in that space, but only certain ones, and only if you are honest about your light.

Here is what grows, how to judge the window you have, where the pots can sit, and a 30-day plan from sowing to the first cut of greens. If you do have a balcony, the companion post on the best vegetables to grow on a balcony covers pot sizes, wind and sun hours.

What vegetables can you grow from seed in an apartment with no balcony?

Leafy greens and small root crops are the vegetables that grow from seed in an apartment window; fruiting crops are not. Kale, spinach, lettuce, beet greens, turnip greens and radishes tolerate the modest light a window gives and fit in a pot under a foot deep. Carrots need a full 12 inches.

The University of Maryland Extension's guide to growing microgreens and baby greens indoors lists beets, kale, mustards, arugula and lettuce among the crops that grow acceptably in less-than-ideal light.

Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplant and melons are a different project. Each needs direct sun for most of the day and space for a plant that can reach several feet. A pepper wants a 3 to 5 gallon container; the others want 5 gallons or more.

A window does not provide that, so these crops get started indoors and finished somewhere outside.

Which window is bright enough to grow vegetables?

A south-facing window with no building directly across from it is the one that grows vegetables; everything else needs help. Illinois Extension's guide to growing herbs and lettuce indoors puts the need at 6 to 8 hours of bright light a day and names southern exposure as the best. East and west windows give roughly half a day of direct sun; a north window gives none.

City windows have a problem suburban windows do not: the building across the street. Stand at the sill at noon and again in mid-afternoon on a clear day and note whether sun is actually hitting the glass. If it is not, treat the window as north-facing no matter which way it points.

Illinois Extension estimates that each hour of natural light takes about 2 hours of supplemental light to replace. Lights should run 14 to 16 hours a day, held 6 to 12 inches above the leaves. A single full-spectrum LED strip on a timer covers one shelf of greens.

Where do the pots go in a small apartment?

The best spot is a sill, a table or a shelf within about a foot of the brightest window, with a tray under every pot. Light drops off quickly as you move back from the glass, so a pot on the far side of the room is effectively in the dark. Rotate each pot a quarter turn every few days so the plants do not lean toward the window.

Weight is rarely a problem indoors, one real advantage over a balcony. Maryland Extension puts dry soilless mix at about 10 pounds per cubic foot, against roughly 75 for garden soil. Even watered, a 12-inch pot of mix is something a sill or a sturdy bookshelf can hold.

The concern in a rental is water, not weight: use saucers deep enough to catch a full watering and never let a pot sit in it.

Radiators are the other apartment constraint: Illinois Extension recommends room temperatures of 70 degrees F and below for greens, kept away from a direct heat source. Use the sill above a radiator for seed starting only. The warm spot speeds germination, but once the seedlings are up they want to move somewhere cooler and bright.

Never use the fire escape. It is an emergency exit, and New York's Multiple Dwelling Law and most other city codes require it to be kept clear of anything that could obstruct it. A pot out there is a violation for your landlord and a hazard for everyone above you.

What containers, soil and water do indoor vegetables need?

Any container with a drainage hole and 6 to 12 inches of depth, filled with bagged potting mix, will grow greens and small roots. Illinois Extension is blunt about the mix: use a quality potting mix and do not use topsoil, which compacts, drains poorly and brings weed seeds and soil pests inside. Fabric pots, plastic nursery pots and window boxes all work; terracotta dries out fastest.

Water when the top inch of mix is dry, and water enough that a little runs into the saucer. Maryland Extension's guide to vegetable seedling care warns that a tray kept constantly wet invites damping-off, the fungus that topples seedlings at the soil line. Dry air from heating makes the opposite mistake easy too, so check pots daily in winter.

Maryland Extension notes that most potting mixes carry enough fertilizer for about 5 to 6 weeks of seedling growth. After that, greens you are cutting repeatedly benefit from a half-strength liquid feed every two weeks, and no more often than that. Microgreens cut at two to three weeks need no fertilizer at all.

A 30-day starter plan for a window garden

Sow spinach and kale on day one, and you will cut the first baby greens around day 25 to 40, sooner under a light. Kale is up in about a week; spinach takes a week or two and prefers a cool room. Both regrow after cutting.

Carrots and beets go in the same day in a deeper pot and simply take longer.

Day What to do What you should see
1 Fill pots, water the mix, sow spinach and kale a half inch deep, 1 inch apart; sow beets and carrots in the deep pot Damp, level mix with the packet date on a label
3 to 10 Keep the surface moist; a sill above a radiator or the top of the fridge speeds things up Spinach and kale sprouts; beets a little later; carrots last
10 to 14 Move sprouted pots to the brightest window or under the light; thin greens to about 2 inches apart Short, sturdy seedlings; if they stretch, they need more light
14 to 21 Water when the top inch is dry; rotate pots; thin carrots to 2 inches First true leaves; beet seedlings in clusters, thin to one
21 to 40 Cut spinach and kale leaves at 4 to 6 inches, leaving the center to regrow A first small harvest; one or two more cuts to come
40 to 60 Mature greens; beets and turnips sized for greens, roots if the light is strong Resow a second pot at day 21 so the supply does not gap

Maryland Extension's timeline matches: baby greens at 4 to 6 inches by day 21 to 40, and mature greens past 6 inches by day 40 to 60. Cut plants give one to two more harvests. Sow a fresh pot every three weeks and the window stays in production.

What about tomatoes and the other warm-season crops?

The warm-season crops are started indoors in the same window and then moved outside, so a no-balcony apartment is a nursery for them, not a home. Sow tomato 6 to 8 weeks and pepper and eggplant 8 weeks before the last spring frost. Cucumber and melon go into warm outdoor soil one to two weeks after it.

Find the date on the state planting calendars and confirm it with your county extension office, because frost dates vary from neighborhood to neighborhood in a city.

Where they go after that is the real question. A community garden plot, a friend's yard, a shared roof the building has approved in writing, or a balcony in your next apartment all work.

For a balcony, check the lease, the building rules and the balcony's weight limit before a wet 5-gallon pot goes out. The post on when to start seeds indoors covers the indoor weeks and hardening off before the move.

If you only ever have the window, grow the five that belong there and give the other five packets to someone with a yard. That is a better outcome than a tomato dying on a sill in June. Common questions

What vegetables can you grow indoors in an apartment without a balcony?

Leafy greens and small roots: kale, spinach, lettuce, beet greens, turnip greens, radishes and carrots in a 12-inch pot. They tolerate the lower light of a window and fit in pots 6 to 12 inches deep. Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers, eggplant and melons need a large container and full outdoor sun, so they are a balcony or community-plot project.

How much light does a windowsill vegetable garden need?

Greens need about 6 to 8 hours of bright light a day, which usually means an unobstructed south-facing window. East and west windows give less; a north window is not enough on its own. Under a grow light, plan on 14 to 16 hours a day with the light 6 to 12 inches above the leaves.

Can you grow vegetables in an apartment with only a north-facing window?

Not well from the window alone. A north window gives soft, indirect light that keeps a houseplant alive but will not grow dense greens. Add an inexpensive full-spectrum LED on a timer for 14 to 16 hours a day and the same greens grow fine on a shelf anywhere in the room.

Can you grow tomatoes indoors in an apartment?

A full-size tomato is not an indoor crop. It needs a 5 gallon or larger container, direct sun for most of the day and room for a plant several feet tall. Start tomato seeds indoors 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost, then move the plant to a balcony, a roof the building allows, or a community plot.

Is it legal to grow plants on a fire escape?

In most cities, no. A fire escape is an emergency exit, and most city fire codes require it to be kept clear of anything that could slow a person climbing down. Pots on a fire escape can bring a violation for your landlord and put neighbors at risk. Keep the garden inside the window or on a real balcony.

When should an apartment gardener start seeds?

Greens for the window can be sown any month, since the room is always warm enough. For tomato, pepper and eggplant, count back 6 to 8 weeks from your last spring frost and sow then; cucumber and melon are sown outdoors a week or two after it. Confirm your frost dates with your county extension office before you plan.
