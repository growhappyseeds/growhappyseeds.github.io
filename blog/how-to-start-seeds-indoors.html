How to Start Seeds Indoors: A Beginner's Step-by-Step Guide

By Happy Seeds · Updated 2026-10-31 · 8 min read

To start seeds indoors, sow them in moist seed-starting mix 6 to 8 weeks before your last frost, cover to 2 to 3 times the seed's width, keep them warm until they sprout, then give them strong light a few inches away and water from below. Harden off for 7 to 10 days before transplanting outside.

Starting seeds indoors is how gardeners in most of the US get tomatoes, peppers and eggplant to ripen before fall. The crops need more warm weeks than the outdoor season provides, so you give them a head start under a window or a cheap light.

When to start seeds indoors

Start seeds indoors by counting back from your average last spring frost date: 6 to 8 weeks for tomatoes, about 8 weeks for peppers and eggplant, and only 3 to 4 weeks for fast growers like melons and squash if you start them at all. Earlier is not better; oversized seedlings stall after transplanting.

Find your last frost date on the planting calendars by state. A gardener in New Jersey with a last frost in late April sows tomatoes in early March; a gardener in much of Texas does the same in January or February.

Containers: anything with drainage

Any clean container 2 to 3 inches deep with drainage holes works for starting seeds, including cell trays, small plastic pots, yogurt cups with holes punched in the bottom and paper pots. Drainage is the only non-negotiable; standing water kills seedlings.

Individual 3-inch pots suit tomatoes and peppers, which spend 6 to 8 weeks indoors and need the root room. Wash reused containers in warm soapy water, because fungus survives in dried soil residue.

Seed-starting mix versus potting soil

Use seed-starting mix rather than potting soil, because it is finer, lighter and sterile, which helps small seeds make contact, lets roots push through easily and reduces damping off. Potting soil works in a pinch but holds more water and often contains chunks that tiny roots struggle around.

Never use garden soil indoors; it compacts, drains poorly and carries fungi and insect eggs. Moisten the mix before filling containers so it feels like a wrung-out sponge, fill the cells, and tap to settle without packing it down.

Sowing depth: 2 to 3 times the seed's width

Sow each seed 2 to 3 times as deep as the seed is wide, which means a quarter inch for tomatoes and peppers, barely covered for tiny seed, and up to an inch for large seed such as melons and squash. Sowing too deep is the most common cause of poor indoor germination.

Sow two seeds per cell and plan to snip the weaker one, or three if the packet's germination rate is below about 75 percent. Cover with a pinch of mix, mist so you do not wash the seed out, and label every cell immediately; tomato and pepper seedlings look alike for weeks. Warmth for germination

Most warm-season seeds germinate fastest with soil at 70 to 85 degrees F, warmer than most rooms in late winter. Tomatoes come up in 5 to 10 days at that temperature; peppers and eggplant can take 10 to 14 days.

The top of a refrigerator, a spot near a heat vent, or a seedling heat mat all work, and a clear dome holds humidity while seeds sprout. Remove the cover as soon as the first seedlings appear, because trapped moisture invites damping off. Once seedlings are up, a cooler 60 to 70 degrees F produces sturdier plants.

Light: a south window or a cheap shop light

Seedlings need 14 to 16 hours of strong light a day, either from a bright south-facing window or from an LED shop light hung 2 to 4 inches above the leaves. Light is where most indoor seed starting goes wrong, because a room that looks bright to us is dim to a plant.

A south window can work for a few trays if you rotate them daily, but in most homes it is not enough in February and March. A basic LED shop light from a hardware store is the simplest fix: hang it on chains so you can raise it as plants grow, and run it on a timer. You do not need a special grow light.

Watering from below

Water seedlings from below by setting their containers in a tray of water for 10 to 20 minutes until the surface darkens, then draining the excess. Bottom watering keeps the surface drier, which discourages fungus, and spares fragile stems.

Water when the top of the mix is dry to the touch, not on a schedule; a dry cell is noticeably lighter when lifted. A small fan on low for a few hours a day strengthens stems, and half-strength liquid fertilizer every two weeks after the first true leaves appear is all the feeding they need.

Hardening off for 7 to 10 days

Harden off seedlings for 7 to 10 days before transplanting by moving them outdoors for increasing periods, starting with an hour or two in shade and ending with full days in sun. Indoor-raised plants have soft tissue that sunburns and tears in wind if moved out all at once.

A typical schedule: days 1 and 2, two hours in a sheltered shady spot; days 3 and 4, half a day with morning sun; days 5 to 7, most of the day in sun; days 8 to 10, all day and overnight if nights stay above 50 degrees F. Water more often during this week, and bring plants in if frost threatens.

Transplanting

Transplant on a cloudy day or in the evening, after the last frost date and once soil is warm, setting each seedling at the depth it grew in its pot. Water the seedlings an hour before, handle them by the leaves rather than the stem, and firm the soil gently around the roots.

Tomatoes are the exception on depth: remove the lower leaves and bury a few inches of stem, which roots along its length. Water well, and shade new transplants for a day or two if the weather turns hot.

For containers, tomatoes want 5 gallons or more, peppers 3 to 5 gallons, and eggplant 5 gallons, which matches the container sizes listed for the kit's varieties on the brand facts page.

Common problems and fixes

The three problems that end most indoor seed-starting attempts are leggy seedlings, damping off and seeds that never come up, and each has a specific cause.

Problem What it looks like Cause Fix
Leggy seedlings Tall, thin, pale stems that flop Too little light, or light too far away; too warm Lower the light to 2 to 4 inches, run it 14 to 16 hours, cool the room, add a fan
Damping off Seedlings collapse at the soil line, stem pinched and dark Fungus thriving in wet, still, humid conditions Remove dome once sprouted, water from below, improve airflow, use sterile mix and clean pots
No germination Nothing after the expected window Sown too deep, soil too cold, mix dried out or waterlogged, old seed Resow shallower on a heat mat, keep mix evenly moist, test seed viability

Leggy seedlings can be saved; damping off usually cannot, so prevention is the whole game. If nothing comes up after twice the expected time, resow rather than wait; early in the season you lose a week, not a harvest.

Common questions

When should I start seeds indoors?

Count back from your average last spring frost date. Tomatoes go in 6 to 8 weeks before it, peppers and eggplant about 8 weeks before. Starting earlier than that produces oversized, stressed seedlings, not an earlier harvest. Your state's frost dates are on the planting calendar pages.

Can I start seeds indoors in regular potting soil?

You can, but seed-starting mix is better. It is finer, lighter and sterile, so tiny seeds make contact with it, roots push through easily, and damping-off fungus is less likely. Potting soil is coarser and often holds more water than seedlings want. Garden soil should not be used indoors.

Do seedlings need a grow light?

A sunny south-facing window can work, but most indoor seedlings get leggy without supplemental light. An inexpensive LED shop light hung 2 to 4 inches above the plants and run 14 to 16 hours a day solves the problem. Raise it as the seedlings grow to keep the gap constant.

Why are my seedlings tall and floppy?

Leggy seedlings are reaching for light. The fix is more light, closer: move them to a brighter spot or lower a shop light to 2 to 4 inches above the leaves. Cooler room temperatures after germination and a small fan for air movement also produce shorter, sturdier stems.

What is hardening off and how long does it take?

Hardening off is the 7 to 10 day process of gradually exposing indoor seedlings to sun, wind and cooler temperatures before transplanting. Start with an hour or two of shade outdoors and add time and sun each day. Skipping it causes sunscald, wilting and transplant shock.

Which vegetables should not be started indoors?

Root crops such as carrots, beets, turnips and radishes resent transplanting and should be direct sown. Beans, peas, cucumbers, squash and corn grow so fast that starting them indoors gains little and their roots dislike disturbance. Spinach and most leafy greens also do best sown directly.
