Radiators, Dry Air and Seedlings in a Heated City Apartment

By Happy Seeds · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Steam heat and forced air make a city apartment warm enough to sprout anything and dry enough to wilt it. The trick is using the heat for one job and dodging it for the next.

The short answer A radiator helps seeds germinate and hurts seedlings afterward. Germinate the tray on a shelf above it, then move sprouted seedlings a few feet away into bright light and raise humidity with grouped pots, a pebble tray or a humidifier.

This guide is for anyone starting vegetable seeds in a heated city apartment, with or without a balcony to finish on. The constraint is the heat itself: a steam radiator or a forced-air vent under the only window, running from October to April, and the dry air it leaves behind. The workable answer is to use that heat for germination and then keep seedlings well away from it.

Heat and humidity pull in opposite directions in a winter apartment. Seeds want a warm, moist mix and do not care about the air. Seedlings want bright light, cooler air and more humidity than a heated apartment ever has, so the plan has two stages in two spots.

Is a radiator good or bad for starting seeds?

A radiator is good for germination and bad for seedlings, so the same heat source helps in week one and harms in week three. Iowa State University Extension's answer on whether you need a heat mat says most seeds germinate best when the medium is consistently 70 to 75 degrees F. It adds that setting containers on top of a radiator or near a heat register usually works fine.

The catch is that the same page is talking about the days before anything sprouts. Once seedlings are up, they need light far more than warmth, and the dry air pouring off the radiator starts working against them. North Dakota State University Extension's winter houseplant care column puts the rule simply: move plants away from the drying warmth of heat sources.

So treat the radiator as a germination appliance. It is a free, steady heat mat for the week or two a tray needs it, and nothing more.

How do you germinate seeds safely near a radiator?

Put the tray on a shelf, board or table a foot or more above the radiator, never on the metal itself, and cover it loosely until sprouts show. Steam radiators cycle on and off, and a tray sitting directly on one can cook the mix during a long cycle. A shelf above it catches the rising warm air without the burn.

Check the mix every morning with a fingertip, because warm moving air dries a small tray in a day. A loose clear cover holds moisture while seeds sprout, and University of Maryland Extension's guide to starting vegetable seeds indoors suggests removing it as soon as sprouts appear. Its slightly cooler target of 65 to 75 degrees F for the potting mix is one a heated apartment reaches without help.

The day the first sprouts show, the tray leaves the radiator for good. The warm spots that work for this stage, including the top of the fridge and an interior bookshelf, are covered in where to start seeds in a small apartment.

Why does heated apartment air hurt seedlings?

Heated air is far drier than seedlings like, and it is moving. Iowa State University Extension's page on raising indoor humidity says the humidity in many homes during winter may be only 10 to 20 percent. Many plants prefer 40 to 50 percent, and the same page lists slow growth and brown leaf tips and edges as the results of air that dry.

A seedling in a small cell has very little soil to buffer that. The mix dries from the top, most of the roots are near the surface, and a plant you watered this morning can flag by evening. Hot air moving past the leaves pulls water out faster than the tiny root system can replace it.

Where should seedlings live once they sprout?

Seedlings belong in the brightest light you have, a few feet back from the radiator, at ordinary room temperature. North Dakota State University Extension gives 65 to 75 degrees F as the range houseplants favor, and seedlings of the kit's cool-season crops are content at the low end. Kale, spinach, beet, carrot and turnip tolerate a cool room far better than they tolerate a hot, dry one.

The windowsill itself is the awkward spot, because the radiator is almost always under it. University of Minnesota Extension's starting seeds indoors notes that a windowsill can be the coldest place in the house at night. With a radiator below it, the same sill is one of the hottest by day, and that swing produces the tall, pale growth described in why apartment seedlings get leggy.

A small table or shelf set back from the window, with a basic lamp over it, is usually the better home. The light is steadier, the air is calmer, and the pots are not sitting in a draft of hot air all afternoon.

How do you raise humidity for seedlings in a dry apartment?

Group the pots, stand them on a pebble tray, or run a small humidifier, and skip the spray bottle. Iowa State University Extension's answer on misting houseplants in a dry home is blunt: misting is not an effective way to raise relative humidity. Foliage dries within minutes, and the page notes you would need to mist several times a day.

The three methods that do work are cheap and apartment-sized. Both Iowa State and North Dakota State list the same three.

A loose clear cover is the exception for seedlings. It works for germination, but a sealed tray of sprouted seedlings in a warm room is the classic setup for damping off. West Virginia University Extension's page on common problems in seedlings ties that collapse to excess water and poor drainage.

Once sprouts are up, the cover comes off and the pots get airflow instead.

Which seeds are worth starting in a heated apartment in winter?

The cool-season five are the ones to sow in a window pot now, and the warm-season five wait for their countdown to last frost. Kale, spinach, beet, carrot and turnip all germinate at ordinary room temperature and grow on in a sunny window or, later, a balcony planter. Their seedlings are also the least bothered by a cool room, which matters if you turn the heat down at night.

Tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant and melon are a different case. They want the warmest spot in the apartment to sprout, and then a big container and real sun to produce anything. A grower with no balcony starts them indoors on time and plans for a roof the building allows or a community plot.

The countdown is in when to start seeds indoors. Confirm your last frost date with your county extension office, or look up your state on the planting pages.

Common questions

Can I start seeds on top of a radiator?

For germination, yes, with care. Iowa State University Extension says containers on top of a radiator or near a heat register usually work. Most seeds germinate best at a steady 70 to 75 degrees F. Keep the tray on a shelf above the radiator rather than on bare metal, and move it to light the day sprouts appear.

Why do my seedlings wilt near the radiator?

Heated air is dry. Iowa State University Extension notes that winter humidity in many homes falls to 10 to 20 percent, while most plants prefer 40 to 50 percent. A radiator also pushes hot, moving air across the leaves, so the potting mix and the seedlings dry out faster than you can water. Move the pots a few feet away.

Does misting seedlings raise the humidity?

Not in any useful way. Iowa State University Extension says misting is not an effective way to raise relative humidity. The foliage dries within minutes, and you would need to mist several times a day. Grouping pots, a pebble tray with the pots above the waterline, or a small humidifier work far better.

Is a windowsill above a radiator a good place for seedlings?

It is one of the hardest spots in the apartment. University of Minnesota Extension points out that a windowsill can be the coldest place in the house at night. A radiator underneath makes the same sill among the hottest by day. That swing stretches seedlings, so use a shelf or table set back from the window and radiator.

What temperature should the room be for seedlings?

Normal room temperature is fine. North Dakota State University Extension gives 65 to 75 degrees F as the range houseplants favor, and apartment seedlings are happy in the same band. Cooler nights are not a problem for kale, spinach, beet, carrot or turnip. Keep seedlings away from the drying warmth of heat sources.

Can the whole vegetable kit grow indoors in winter?

No. The five cool-season packets, kale, spinach, carrot, beet and turnip, grow in a sunny window or a balcony planter. The warm-season five, tomato, cucumber, pepper, eggplant and melon, need a big container and real sun. A no-balcony grower starts them indoors and needs a balcony, a roof the building allows or a community plot later.
