How Many Seeds Are in a Seed Packet? Counts by Vegetable

By Happy Seeds · November 14, 2026 · 7 min read

What a packet of each common vegetable holds, why the number swings from a dozen to a thousand, and how to tell whether a packet is enough for the garden you are planning.

The short answer A home-garden seed packet holds anywhere from about 10 seeds to a thousand or more, because packets are filled by weight or by count and seed size varies by crop. A gram of carrot seed is about 700 seeds; a gram of cucumber seed is about 35.

A seed packet can hold a dozen seeds or a thousand, and the packet rarely looks any different from the outside. That matters when you are planning a first garden, because the question behind "how many seeds are in a packet" is really "is this packet enough".

Here is what a packet of each common vegetable holds, why the counts swing so widely, and how many seeds a home garden actually uses.

How many seeds are in a seed packet?

A home-garden packet holds between about 10 seeds and a thousand or more, and the count depends on the crop more than the brand, because seed size varies so much. Osborne Quality Seeds' seed count chart puts carrot at about 18,750 seeds per ounce and cucumber at about 940. A packet of the same weight holds twenty times as many carrot seeds.

The table gives working figures from two supplier charts, Osborne's per-ounce counts and True Leaf Market's seeds per gram. Both say the numbers are approximations that vary by variety and seed lot.

Vegetable Seeds per gram (approx.) Seeds per ounce (approx.) Seed size
Carrot 700 18,750 Dust-fine
Turnip 335 11,900 Tiny
Kale 280 6,250 Tiny, round
Tomato 265 10,300 Small, flat
Eggplant 230 6,250 Small, flat
Pepper 175 3,125 Small, flat
Spinach 75 2,500 Medium
Beet 55 1,875 Medium, a cluster of seeds
Melon 40 1,250 Large, flat
Cucumber 35 940 Large, flat

Read the two columns as a range, not a contradiction. Osborne counts about 10,300 tomato seeds per ounce while True Leaf gives 7,500 for a beefsteak type and 10,000 for a cherry, and both are right for different varieties. A gram is enough to tell you whether a packet is generous.

Why do seed packets hold such different numbers?

Packets differ because some are filled by weight and some by count, and because the seller decides how much to include. Botanical Interests explains on its help page that it packs some seeds by weight with an approximate count printed, and others by an exact count. Fine seed like carrot is usually weighed; big seed like cucumber is easy to count.

Generosity varies more than most buyers expect. The Laidback Gardener's post on seed packs notes that most suppliers include more seed than a home gardener needs in a season, while a few are stingy. Its example is a supplier that puts only five or six tomato seeds in a pack.

Hybrid and specialty varieties often come in smaller counts because the seed costs more to produce.

Nebraska Extension's guide to understanding the seed packet adds the practical point: knowing how many seeds are in a package helps greatly when ordering seed and planning a garden. Look for the count or the weight on the packet, and if you see only a weight, the table above turns it into a number. Our guide to reading a seed packet covers the rest of the label.

How many seeds do you actually need?

A home garden needs far fewer seeds than most packets hold, especially for the vegetables you transplant. University of Missouri Extension's vegetable planting calendar suggests three to five tomato plants, two to three pepper plants and two to three eggplant plants per person for fresh eating. Sow two or three seeds per plant you want and the whole family's tomatoes come out of one packet.

Direct-sown crops use more seed because you sow thickly and thin. Johnny's Selected Seeds' direct-seeding chart gives average seeds per 100 feet of row, and dividing by ten gives a realistic home-garden row.

Vegetable Seeds per 100 ft row (Johnny's) Seeds for a 10 ft row Row per person (Missouri)
Carrot 3,000 300 5 to 10 ft
Beet 1,500 150 5 to 10 ft
Spinach 1,000 100 5 to 10 ft
Kale 450 45 5 to 10 ft
Cucumber 200 20 2 to 3 hills
Melon 200 20 3 to 5 hills

Missouri lists turnip at half an ounce of seed per 100 feet, which at roughly 335 seeds per gram is several thousand seeds. A 10-foot row therefore takes a few hundred.

In containers the numbers shrink again. A 12-inch pot holds a couple of dozen carrots or about ten beets, and a balcony planter of kale needs a handful of seeds, not sixty. Thin to the spacing on the packet and the surplus is next year's sowing.

Is 100 seeds a packet enough for a home garden?

Yes, for a short row or a set of containers of every common vegetable, and for the transplanted crops it is enough for two or three seasons. Run the numbers: a 10-foot row of spinach uses about 100 seeds and a row of kale under 50. A family of four needs 12 to 20 tomato plants on Missouri's figures, which is 24 to 40 seeds at two per plant.

Carrot is the crop most likely to use a whole packet in one long row. It is also the one whose packets hold the most seed, because the seed is so small.

What should you do with the seeds you do not sow?

Keep them cool, dry and closed up, and most will sow again next year. Nebraska Extension's packet guide says the point of a dry, closed container is to stop the seed absorbing moisture from the air, which shortens its viability. A jar in a cool closet does more for seed life than any fancy box.

Osborne's seed viability chart lists tomato, cucumber and melon among the longer keepers and carrot among the shorter, with every figure depending on storage.

Treat an old packet as a question, not a loss: sow ten seeds on a damp paper towel and count what sprouts in one to two weeks. Then sow a little thicker to make up the difference. Common questions

How many seeds are in a packet of tomato seeds?

Typically a few dozen for a home-garden packet, though some suppliers include far more and a few include only five or six. Tomato seed is small, around 265 seeds per gram for a beefsteak type, so even a light packet holds more plants than most gardens can place.

How many seeds are in a packet of carrot seeds?

Often several hundred to more than a thousand, because carrot seed is tiny, roughly 700 seeds per gram and around 19,000 per ounce. Carrot packets are almost always filled by weight, and the printed count is an estimate rather than a tally.

Are seed packets sold by weight or by count?

Both, depending on the crop. Botanical Interests, for example, packs some seeds by weight and prints an approximate count, and packs others by an exact count. Large seeds like cucumber, melon and beet are easy to count; dust-fine seeds like carrot and turnip are weighed.

Is 100 seeds per packet enough for a home garden?

Yes, for almost every crop. A 10-foot row needs roughly 300 carrot seeds, 150 beet seeds, 100 spinach seeds, 45 kale seeds and 20 cucumber seeds. A family needs only two to five tomato, pepper or eggplant plants per person. The extra seed covers thinning, a second sowing and next year.

How many tomato plants does a family need?

University of Missouri Extension suggests three to five tomato plants per person for fresh eating and five to ten if you also preserve. Sow two or three seeds for every plant you want, keep the strongest seedling, and the rest of the packet keeps for a later season.

Do leftover seeds keep until next year?

Most do, if they stay cool and dry in a closed container. Nebraska Extension notes that moisture from the air is what shortens seed life. Supplier viability charts list tomato, cucumber and melon among the longer keepers and carrot among the shorter, with every figure depending on storage.
