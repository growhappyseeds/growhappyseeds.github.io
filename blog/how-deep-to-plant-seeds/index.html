How Deep to Plant Vegetable Seeds: The Rule and a Crop Chart

By Happy Seeds · November 20, 2026 · 7 min read

The two-to-three-times rule, what happens when you plant too deep or too shallow, how soil and season change the depth, and a chart for ten common vegetables.

The short answer Plant a vegetable seed two to three times as deep as it is wide: a quarter to a half inch for carrot or spinach, up to an inch for cucumber. When in doubt, plant shallower, because a seed buried too deep runs out of energy before it reaches light.

Most vegetable seeds go in the ground two to three times as deep as they are wide, and the common mistake is burying them deeper than that. A carrot seed the size of a pinhead wants a quarter inch of soil over it. A cucumber seed the size of a fingernail wants about an inch.

Most other vegetables fall between those two, with corn, beans and squash going deeper still.

That one rule covers most of the work, but the exceptions matter: heavy clay, sandy soil, midsummer sowing and a handful of seeds that want light. The sections below take each in turn and end with a chart for ten common vegetables.

What is the rule for planting depth?

Cover a seed two to three times its own width, which is the rule the University of Georgia Extension gives in its note on planting depth and soil temperature. Small seeds like carrots are planted very shallow, and larger seeds like corn or beans go an inch or deeper. The width that matters is the seed's narrow dimension, not its length.

Virginia Tech's guide Seed for the Garden states the same idea with a deeper multiplier, four to five times the seed's width, and then gives the practical result.

Most seeds should be planted a quarter to a half inch deep, and small seeds such as celery only an eighth of an inch. Vine crops, sweet corn and beans go an inch or deeper. It adds the line every gardener should remember: there are exceptions, so read the packet.

For indoor sowing, University of Maryland Extension's guide to starting vegetable seeds indoors uses about twice the seed's diameter. Its worked example is a seed an eighth of an inch wide planted a quarter inch deep, which describes tomato, pepper and eggplant seed well.

What happens if you plant seeds too deep?

A seed planted too deep uses up its stored food before the shoot reaches light, so it never emerges or arrives weak and late. The University of Georgia note explains it plainly: the seed only has so much energy to grow through the soil, and a deep seed struggles to reach the sunlight. A row that never comes up is worth checking for depth before blaming the packet.

Depth also interacts with soil temperature and moisture. Deeper soil is colder and wetter in spring, so a seed buried too far sits in cold, damp ground for longer and is more likely to rot. The smaller the seed, the less margin it has, which is why carrots, spinach and kale stay at the shallow end of their range.

If a sowing fails, check depth first and the packet's germination rate second. Every Happy Seeds packet prints its lab-tested germination rate, and how to read a seed packet explains what that number means for how many seeds to sow.

What happens if you plant seeds too shallow?

A seed planted too shallow dries out, sprouts unevenly or washes away in the first hard watering. The University of Georgia note calls shallow planting the lesser problem of the two, but says such seeds might dry out and have a tough time germinating. The fix is not to plant deeper; it is to keep the surface evenly moist until the seedlings are up.

Firming the soil over the row solves most of this. Virginia Tech says firming soil so it is in direct contact with the seed improves water uptake and hastens germination.

For tiny seeds, Nebraska Extension's guide to starting seeds indoors suggests dusting them on the surface and lightly pressing them in. Water from below so the seeds are not buried deeper by the water.

How do soil and season change the depth?

Plant a little deeper in sandy or loose soil and in midsummer, and a little shallower in heavy clay. Virginia Tech's guide says seeds may be planted deeper in sandy or mellow soil with plenty of organic matter. Loose soil is easy to push through and dries fast at the surface.

In heavy clay or silt that crusts over, it says to cover seeds only two to three times their diameter.

The same guide gives a midsummer rule for fall crops: cover the seeds six to eight times their diameter. Warm, dry soil at the surface would otherwise kill the sprout.

That is the depth to use when sowing the kit's cool-season five for fall. Timing for those sowings, and your frost dates, should be confirmed with your county extension office or on the state planting pages.

How deep to plant each vegetable: a chart for ten crops

The ten crops in a first vegetable garden sort into three depths: a quarter inch, a half inch and an inch. The chart uses Missouri Extension's vegetable planting calendar and Illinois Extension's planting dates, spacing and seeds for the direct-sown crops. Where the two differ, the chart shows the range, and the shallow end is the safer bet.

Eggplant About 1/4 inch Small, flat Started indoors, same as pepper
Cucumber 1/2 to 1 inch Large, flat Direct sow after frost once soil is warm
Melon 1/2 to 1 inch Large, flat Direct sow after frost, or indoors 3 weeks before transplanting

The tomato, pepper and eggplant figures come from the Maryland twice-the-width rule for indoor sowing rather than from a field chart, since those three are almost always transplanted. For the warm-season crops, when to sow matters as much as how deep, and when to start seeds indoors gives the countdown from your last frost date.

How do you measure a quarter inch in the garden?

Draw the furrow with a pencil laid on its side for the shallow crops, and use a fingertip pushed in to the first joint for the deep ones. A pencil pressed partway into loose soil leaves a shallow groove, roughly a quarter inch, which suits carrot, spinach, kale and turnip. A finger pushed in to the first joint makes a hole close to an inch, right for cucumber and melon.

For beets, push the pencil's eraser end in about a half inch. Indoors, the Maryland guide describes shallow rows an eighth to a quarter inch deep in the seed-starting mix. That is all the planning a tray of tomato or pepper seed needs.

Sow, cover lightly, firm the surface, and water gently so the seeds stay where you put them.

Does seed depth matter more for small gardens and containers?

Yes, a little, because potting mix dries faster at the surface than garden soil, so the shallow end of each range needs steadier watering. The rule itself does not change in a pot or raised bed. Carrot seed still goes a quarter inch deep in a 12-inch container, and cucumber still goes about an inch deep in a 5-gallon pot with a trellis.

Common questions

What is the general rule for how deep to plant seeds?

Cover a seed two to three times its width, which is the rule University of Georgia Extension gives. Virginia Tech's seed guide says most vegetable seeds end up a quarter to a half inch deep, and small seeds like celery an eighth of an inch. Vine crops, corn and beans go an inch or deeper. The packet lists any exceptions.

What happens if you plant seeds too deep?

The seedling runs out of stored energy before it reaches the surface and never emerges, or comes up weak and late. University of Georgia Extension calls planting too deep the bigger risk, since a seed has only so much food to push a shoot through soil. If a row fails to come up, depth is the first thing to check.

What happens if you plant seeds too shallow?

They dry out. A seed sitting near the surface can sprout and then die when the top quarter inch of soil dries between waterings. University of Georgia Extension says shallow planting is less of a problem than deep planting, as long as you keep the surface evenly moist until the seedlings are up.

How deep do you plant carrot seeds?

About a quarter inch, with some guides going to a half inch. Missouri Extension lists a quarter inch for carrots, and Illinois Extension lists a half inch. Carrot seed is tiny and slow, so sow at the shallow end, firm the soil over the row, and keep it moist for two to three weeks until it emerges.

How deep do you plant tomato and pepper seeds indoors?

About a quarter inch. University of Maryland Extension's rule for indoor sowing is twice the seed's width, so a seed an eighth of an inch across goes a quarter inch deep. Tomato, pepper and eggplant seeds are all in that size range. Press the mix lightly over them and water from below so they stay put.

Do any vegetable seeds need light to germinate?

A few do. Virginia Tech's seed guide notes that some seeds require light to germinate and should not be covered at all. University of Maryland Extension says lettuce can be left on the surface or very lightly covered. The ten crops in the Happy Seeds kit all germinate fine under a thin layer of soil.
